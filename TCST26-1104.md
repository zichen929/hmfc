# TCST26
## Scope

- Vertical 2-D planar robot.
- Frictionless, rigid, unilateral contact against a fixed planar surface / line.
- Analysis starts with contact already established and zero normal velocity. Contact establishment, impacts, and contact-mode transitions are outside scope.
- Known robot kinematics & dynamics.
- Robot joint angle and angular velocity noise-free measurement. 
- Exact scalar normal-force measurement $f_m=f\geq0$.
- Regulation. Tracking is a possible extension and is modeled, but not required.  

## Modeling

### Robot Manipulator

Consider a 2-DoF robot manipulator constrained to move in a vertical plane. The joint-space dynamics is
$$
\begin{align}
M(q)\ddot q + C(q,\dot q)\dot q + G(q)
=
\tau + \tau_e .
\end{align}
$$
The joint position is $q\in\mathbb R^{2}$, $M(q)\in\mathbb R^{2\times 2}$ is symmetric positive definite, $C(q,\dot q)\dot q$ contains the Coriolis and centrifugal terms, $G(q)$ is the generally configuration-dependent gravity vector for motion in the vertical plane, $\tau$ is the actuator torque, and $\tau_e$ is the external torque induced by contact.

The robot kinematic and dynamic models, including $h(q)$, $J(q)$, $M(q)$, $C(q,\dot q)$, and $G(q)$, are assumed to be exactly known.

We assume that the inertial frame and robot-base frame coincide and denote this frame by $B$, with its first axis horizontal and its second axis vertically upward. The gravitational acceleration expressed in $B$ is $g_B=[0\;-g]^T$, where $g>0$. Let
$$
\begin{align}
p=h(q)\in\mathbb R^{2}
\end{align}
$$
be the end-effector position expressed in $B$. The end-effector velocity is
$$
\begin{align}
\dot p = J(q)\dot q
\end{align}
$$
Assume that $J(q)\in\mathbb R^{2\times 2}$ is nonsingular throughout the operating region. Then $J^{-1}(q)$ exists, and the task-space Cartesian robot dynamics can be written as
$$
\begin{align}
\Lambda(q)\ddot p+\eta(q,\dot q)=F+F_e,
\end{align}
$$
where
$$
\begin{align}
\Lambda &= J^{-T}MJ^{-1}, \quad \Lambda=\Lambda^T \succ {0} \\
\eta &= J^{-T}(C\dot q+G)-\Lambda\dot J J^{-1}\dot p,
\end{align}
$$
$F = J^{-T}\tau$ is the commanded force and $F_e=J^{-T}\tau_e$ is the physical contact force exerted on the end-effector; gravity is included in $\eta$. 

We assume 
$$
\begin{align}
0<\underline m I_2
\preceq
\Lambda(q)
\preceq
\overline m I_2
\end{align}
$$
throughout a compact nonsingular operating region. Since $q$ and $\dot q$ are measured, and $J(q)$ is assumed nonsingular, the end-effector position and velocity can be updated with $q$ and $\dot q$ measurements.

### Interaction Force

For rigid contact in the vertical plane, the physical interaction force model is
$$
\begin{align}
F_{e}=F_{n}+F_{t}=nf+F_{t},
\end{align}
$$
where $n\in S^{1}$ is the surface unit normal and $f=n^{T}F_{e}$ is the scalar normal force magnitude. The sign of $n$ is assumed to be fixed according to the contact convention. In particular, $n$ is chosen to point from the environment toward the robot/free-space side, so that under unilateral contact a compressive normal contact force can be written as
$$
\begin{align}
F_{n}=nf, \qquad f\geq 0.
\end{align}
$$
Here $f$ is the compressive normal-force magnitude. The unilateral surface can exert a compressive reaction but cannot exert a tensile reaction.

The tangential frictional $F_{t}$ force satisfies $n^{T}F_{t}=0$, hence we can write $F_{t}=QF_{e}$, where $Q$ is the tangential subspace projector. In frictionless contact case, $F_{t}=0$,
$$
\begin{align}
F_{e}=F_{n}=nf.
\end{align}
$$
Thus frictionless contact is a special case of the same physical model.

We assume that the force sensor can either measure the physical force vector or the scalar normal force and denote these measurements as $F_{m}$ and $f_{m}$, respectively. When exact scalar force feedback is assumed, we have $f_{m}=f$.

In the frictionless case with $f>0$, exact full vector measurement of $F_{e}$ is not assumed, since
$$
\begin{align}
F_{m}=F_{e}=nf
\end{align}
$$
would immediately give
$$
\begin{align}
n=\frac{F_{e}}{\|F_{e}\|}.
\end{align}
$$
Hence, under the joint conditions of frictionless contact and $f>0$, the surface normal would be algebraically known and geometry adaptation would become unnecessary. The expression $n=F_e/\|F_e\|$ is not used when friction is present or when $f=0$.

### Contact Surface

The fixed contact surface is modeled as a straight line of arbitrary orientation in the vertical workspace
$$
\begin{align}
\mathcal{S} =
\left\{
p\in\mathbb{R}^{2} \ |\ \phi(p)=n^{T}(p-p_{0})=0
\right\},
\end{align}
$$
where $n\in S^{1}$ is a constant unit normal vector. Analysis begins after contact has already been established, and the controller-initialization time is $t_c=0$. The surface-frame origin is chosen as the contact position at initialization, i.e.
$$
\begin{align}
p_{0}=p(t_{c})=p(0), \qquad t_c=0,
\end{align}
$$
After initialization, $p_{0}$ is kept fixed.

When the rigid contact is maintained, 
$$
\begin{align}
n^T\dot p=0,
\qquad
n^T\ddot p=0.
\end{align}
$$
We assume the initial condition satisfies 
$$
\begin{align}
\phi(p(0)) & = 0, \\
n^{T}\dot p(0) & = 0.
\end{align}
$$
The unilateral contact conditions are
$$
\begin{align}
\phi(p)&\geq0,\qquad f\geq0,\qquad f\phi(p)=0.
\end{align}
$$
The constrained dynamics below describe the active-contact branch $\phi(p)=0$ with zero normal velocity. A strictly positive calculated reaction certifies that this branch is compatible with unilateral contact. The controller analysis verifies this condition for the stated initial conditions and operating domain; the model does not include contact establishment, impacts, or transitions to other contact modes.

We define the tangential projector as
$$
\begin{align}
Q = I_2-nn^T.
\end{align}
$$
Define the tangent unit vector
$$
\begin{align}
T=T(n)=R_{\text{-90}}n\in\mathbb R^2,
\qquad
R_{\text{-90}}=
\begin{bmatrix}
0&1\\
-1&0
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{align}
$$
The clockwise $90^\circ$ rotation fixes the tangent orientation uniquely for each unit normal. The tangent vector satisfies
$$
\begin{align}
T^TT=1,\qquad T^Tn=0,\qquad TT^T=Q.
\end{align}
$$

The contact surface frame is defined by the rotation matrix
$$
\begin{align}
R_{s}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
T & n
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{align}
$$
Since the two basis vectors are mutually orthonormal and right-handed,
$$
\begin{align}
R_{s}^{T}R_{s}=I_2,
\qquad
\det R_{s}=1,
\end{align}
$$
and therefore
$$
\begin{align}
R_{s}\in SO(2).
\end{align}
$$
The corresponding contact-frame coordinates of the end-effector are
$$
\begin{align}
x
=
R_{s}^{T}(p-p_{0})
=
\begin{bmatrix}
x_{t} \\
x_{n}
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{align}
$$
Under rigid maintained contact,
$$
\begin{align}
x_{n}
=
n^{T}(p-p_{0})
\equiv 0.
\end{align}
$$
Hence the motion on the surface is completely represented by the intrinsic 1-D coordinate
$$
\begin{align}
x_{t} = T^{T}(p-p_{0}) \in\mathbb{R}.
\end{align}
$$
Since the contact line is fixed, $T$ is constant, and
$$
\begin{align}
p&=p_0+Tx_{t},\\
\dot p&=T\dot x_{t},\\
\ddot p&=T\ddot x_{t}.
\end{align}
$$

### Motion-Force Decoupled Dynamics

Using $T$, the tangential force can be written as
$$
\begin{align}
F_{t} = Tf_{t},
\end{align}
$$
where $f_{t}=T^T F_{t} \in \mathbb{R}$ is the tangential force expressed along the tangent $T$. Note that in the frictionless case $f_{t}=0$. In the general case,
$$
\begin{align}
F_{e}=nf+Tf_{t},
\end{align}
$$
Substituting $F_{e}$ into the task-space Cartesian dynamics yields
$$
\begin{align}
\Lambda T\ddot x_{t}+\eta
=
F+nf+Tf_{t}.
\end{align}
$$
Projecting this dynamics onto the tangent space yields 
$$
\begin{align}
T^{T}\Lambda T\ddot x_{t}
=
T^{T}(F-\eta)+f_{t}.
\end{align}
$$
Define the scalar tangential inertia $\Lambda_{t}=T^{T}\Lambda T>0$. Then
$$
\begin{align}
\Lambda_{t}\ddot x_{t}
=
T^{T}(F-\eta)+f_{t}.
\end{align}
$$
Projecting the same task-space dynamics onto the normal direction gives
$$
\begin{align}
f
=
n^{T}\Lambda T\ddot x_{t}
-
n^{T}(F-\eta).
\end{align}
$$
Using the acceleration constraint on the contact line
$$
\begin{align}
n^{T}\ddot p=0,
\end{align}
$$
we obtain
$$
\begin{align}
f
=
-
\frac{
n^{T}\Lambda^{-1}(F-\eta+F_{t})
}{
n^{T}\Lambda^{-1}n
}.
\end{align}
$$
In frictionless case, 
$$
\begin{align}
f
=
-
\frac{
n^{T}\Lambda^{-1}(F-\eta)
}{
n^{T}\Lambda^{-1}n
}.
\end{align}
$$

## Full-Information Control (FIC)
### Reference Architecture 

For regulation problems, the desired tangential position can either be given as the task-space reference $p_{d} \in \mathbb{R}^2$, or as the one-dimensional intrinsic reference in the vertical plane $x_{t,d}\in\mathbb{R}$. When the true surface geometry is known, $T=T(n)$ is constant, $p_{d}$ and $x_{t,d}$ are related by the surface geometry
$$
\begin{align}
p_{d} & = p_{0}+Tx_{t,d}.
\end{align}
$$
The desired constant normal force is 
$$
\begin{align}
f_{d}>0.
\end{align}
$$
For tracking problems, $p_{d}(t)$ and $x_{t,d}(t)$ are time-varying.  The corresponding relationship and derivatives are
$$
\begin{align}
p_{d} & = p_{0}+Tx_{t,d}, \\
\dot p_{d} & = T\dot x_{t,d}, \\
\ddot p_{d} & = T\ddot x_{t,d},
\end{align}
$$
 where the time-dependencies are removed for notation simplicity. 
 
 The time-varying force tracking is out of the scope of this work. 

### Error Coordinate

The motion error can be expressed in task-space coordinates or in the one-dimensional surface tangential coordinate. The corresponding errors are denoted by $e_p$ and $e_t$, respectively. Define
$$
\begin{align}
e_p&=p_{d}-p\in\mathbb R^2,\\
e_t&=x_{t,d}-x_t\in\mathbb R.
\end{align}
$$
With known geometry and under maintained contact, both $p$ and $p_d$ lie on the true contact line. Since $p=p_0+Tx_t$, the two error representations satisfy
$$
\begin{align}
e_p&=Te_t,\\
e_t&=T^Te_p,\\
Qe_p&=e_p,\qquad n^Te_p=0.
\end{align}
$$
Given that $T^TT=1$,
$$
\begin{align}
\|e_p\|^2=e_t^2.
\end{align}
$$
Consequently, $e_p\to0$ and $e_t\to0$ describe the same motion-tracking objective. The normal component of the position error is identically zero under the rigid-contact constraint. 

The contact line and its tangent $T$ are fixed. For regulation, $p_d$ and $x_{t,d}$ are constant, so
$$
\begin{align}
\dot e_p&=-\dot p,\qquad \ddot e_p=-\ddot p,\\
\dot e_t&=-\dot x_t,\qquad \ddot e_t=-\ddot x_t.
\end{align}
$$
For a time-varying reference,
$$
\begin{align}
\dot e_p&=\dot p_d-\dot p=T\dot e_t,\\
\dot e_t&=\dot x_{t,d}-\dot x_t,\\
\ddot e_p&=\ddot p_d-\ddot p=T\ddot e_t,\\
\ddot e_t&=\ddot x_{t,d}-\ddot x_t.
\end{align}
$$
The normal interaction is regulated through the force error. For the constant desired normal force $f_d>0$, define
$$
\begin{align}
e_f=f_d-f.
\end{align}
$$
When exact scalar force feedback is available, $f_m=f$, and the same error is computed as
$$
\begin{align}
e_f=f_d-f_m.
\end{align}
$$
The combined motion-force objective is $e_t\to0$ and $e_f\to0$, equivalently $e_p\to0$ and $e_f\to0$.
### Nominal Motion-Force Control

Define the tangential task-space acceleration command $u_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^2$, its scalar tangential-subspace representation $u_{t}\in\mathbb{R}$, and the physical normal-force command $u_{f}\in\mathbb{R}$. The two acceleration-command representations satisfy
$$
\begin{align}
u_{p}=Tu_{t},\qquad u_{t}=T^Tu_{p},\qquad n^Tu_{p}=0.
\end{align}
$$
When the real surface geometry is known, choose a nominal controller with exact dynamic and gravity compensation through $\eta$
$$
\begin{align}
F = \eta + \Lambda u_{p} - Tf_{t} - nu_{f}
= \eta + \Lambda T u_{t} - Tf_{t} - nu_{f}.
\end{align}
$$
For frictionless contact, the nominal controller becomes
$$
\begin{align}
F = \eta + \Lambda u_{p} - nu_{f}
= \eta + \Lambda T u_{t} - nu_{f}.
\end{align}
$$
Substituting the controller into the task-space dynamics yields the nominal closed-loop
$$
\begin{align}
\ddot p&=u_{p}, \\
\ddot x_{t}&=u_{t}, \\
f&=u_{f}.
\end{align}
$$
The explicit realization of $u_{p}$, equivalently $u_{t}$, and $u_{f}$ depends on specific controller designs. For initially established unilateral contact, a strictly positive reaction $f=u_f>0$ makes this constrained solution admissible and maintains contact. The FIC 1–4 designs below give $f=f_d>0$ in the ideal model, so their established-contact operation is compatible with the unilateral contact assumption.

### FIC 1: Task-Space Tracking

This full-information controller follows a twice continuously differentiable task-space reference $p_d(t)$ on the fixed true contact line. The signals $p_d$, $\dot p_d$, and $\ddot p_d$ are known, and the desired normal force remains the constant $f_d>0$. The Cartesian motion errors are $e_p=p_d-p$ and $\dot e_p=\dot p_d-\dot p$. The frictionless rigid unilateral contact and exact scalar force measurement $f_m=f$ specified in the Scope are assumed throughout.

Choose scalar gains $k_p>0$, $k_v>0$, and $k_f\geq0$ for both controller realizations below. Regulation is the constant-reference special case of tracking, and both realizations yield the same closed-loop system in error coordinates.

#### Regulator

For a constant desired point $p_d$ on the contact line, $\dot p_d=\ddot p_d=0$ and $\dot e_p=-\dot p$. Define
$$
\begin{align}
u_{p}&=k_pe_p-k_v\dot p,\\
u_{f}&=f_d+k_fe_f,\qquad e_f=f_d-f_m,\\
F&=\eta+\Lambda\left(k_pe_p-k_v\dot p\right)-nu_{f},\\
\tau&=J^TF.
\end{align}
$$
Since $n^Te_p=0$ and $n^T\dot p=0$ under maintained contact, $n^Tu_{p}=0$. Thus this Cartesian control law realizes the nominal tangential acceleration command without computing $x_t$ or $x_{t,d}$.

#### Tracking Controller

For a time-varying reference $p_d(t)$ on the contact line, define
$$
\begin{align}
u_{p}&=\ddot p_d+k_v\dot e_p+k_pe_p,\\
u_{f}&=f_d+k_fe_f,\qquad e_f=f_d-f_m,\\
F&=\eta+\Lambda\left(\ddot p_d+k_v\dot e_p+k_pe_p\right)-nu_{f},\\
\tau&=J^TF.
\end{align}
$$
The reference acceleration $\ddot p_d$ provides motion feedforward, while the position and velocity error terms provide feedback. The $\eta$ term compensates the known robot dynamics, including gravity in the vertical plane. The choice $k_f=0$ gives the feedforward normal-force command $u_{f}=f_d$.

Since the reference lies on the fixed contact line, $n^T\dot p_d=0$ and $n^T\ddot p_d=0$. Under maintained contact, $n^Te_p=0$ and $n^T\dot e_p=0$, so
$$
\begin{align}
n^Tu_{p}=n^T\left(\ddot p_d+k_v\dot e_p+k_pe_p\right)=0.
\end{align}
$$
Consequently, the task-space command is tangential and realizes the nominal motion-force controller with
$$
\begin{align}
u_{t}&=T^Tu_{p}
=\ddot x_{t,d}+k_v\dot e_t+k_pe_t,\\
Tu_{t}&=Qu_{p}=u_{p}.
\end{align}
$$
These relations identify the intrinsic command for the analysis; the implemented Cartesian force law uses $p_d$, $\dot p_d$, and $\ddot p_d$ directly without computing $x_t$ or $x_{t,d}$.

#### Closed-Loop Error Dynamics

For the tracking controller, substitution into the nominal closed-loop equations $\ddot p=u_{p}$, $\ddot x_t=u_{t}$, and $f=u_{f}$ gives
$$
\begin{align}
\ddot p&=\ddot p_d+k_v\dot e_p+k_pe_p,\\
f&=f_d+k_f(f_d-f).
\end{align}
$$
Using $\ddot e_p=\ddot p_d-\ddot p$ and rearranging yields
$$
\begin{align}
\ddot e_p+k_v\dot e_p+k_pe_p&=0,\\
(1+k_f)e_f&=0.
\end{align}
$$
For the regulation controller, $\ddot p_d=0$ and $\dot e_p=-\dot p$ give the same error equations. Hence the following analysis applies to both controller realizations.

Equivalently, projection onto the fixed tangent gives
$$
\begin{align}
\ddot e_t+k_v\dot e_t+k_pe_t=0.
\end{align}
$$
#### Stability for Regulation and Tracking

Each Cartesian motion-error component satisfies the characteristic equation
$$
\begin{align}
\mu^2+k_v\mu+k_p=0.
\end{align}
$$
Since $k_p>0$ and $k_v>0$, both roots have strictly negative real parts. Thus the motion-error equilibrium $(e_p,\dot e_p)=(0,0)$ is exponentially stable on the maintained-contact state space, and
$$
\begin{align}
p(t)-p_d(t)&\to0,\\
\dot p(t)-\dot p_d(t)&\to0,\\
e_t(t)&\to0.
\end{align}
$$
Since $1+k_f>0$, the algebraic force-error relation gives $e_f=0$, or $f=f_d$, exactly in the ideal rigid-contact model. Both controllers therefore achieve exponential convergence of the motion errors and exact regulation of the constant normal force while the trajectory remains in the nonsingular operating region and rigid contact is maintained. For a constant reference, this gives $p\to p_d$ and $\dot p\to0$; for a time-varying reference, it gives $p-p_d\to0$ and $\dot p-\dot p_d\to0$.

### FIC 2: Task-Space Tracking with Intrinsic Reference

This full-information controller receives the intrinsic reference $x_{t,d}$ and converts it into a task-space reference before applying the Cartesian control law of FIC 1. The true geometry $n$, $T=T(n)$, and the fixed contact point $p_0$ are known. The desired normal force is the constant $f_d>0$, and the frictionless rigid unilateral contact and exact scalar force measurement $f_m=f$ specified in the Scope are assumed throughout. Choose scalar gains $k_p>0$, $k_v>0$, and $k_f\geq0$ for both controller realizations below.

Despite its mathematical equivalency to FIC 1, this controller is listed separately for future purposes. 

#### Reference Conversion

For tracking, let $x_{t,d}(t)$ be twice continuously differentiable, with known first and second derivatives. Since the contact line is fixed, $p_0$ and $T$ are constant, and the converted reference is
$$
\begin{align}
p_d&=p_0+Tx_{t,d},\\
\dot p_d&=T\dot x_{t,d},\\
\ddot p_d&=T\ddot x_{t,d}.
\end{align}
$$
The converted $p_d$ lies on the true contact line, and $n^T\dot p_d=n^T\ddot p_d=0$. The Cartesian errors used by the controller are
$$
\begin{align}
e_p&=p_0+Tx_{t,d}-p,\\
\dot e_p&=T\dot x_{t,d}-\dot p.
\end{align}
$$
Under maintained contact, $e_p=Te_t$ and $\dot e_p=T\dot e_t$, where $e_t=x_{t,d}-x_t$. These identities are used for the analysis; implementation requires the converted Cartesian reference and measured $p,\dot p$, without computing the actual intrinsic coordinate $x_t$.

#### Regulator

For a constant intrinsic reference $x_{t,d}$, the converted $p_d=p_0+Tx_{t,d}$ is constant. The regulation controller is
$$
\begin{align}
u_{p}&=k_p\left(p_0+Tx_{t,d}-p\right)-k_v\dot p,\\
u_{f}&=f_d+k_fe_f,\qquad e_f=f_d-f_m,\\
F&=\eta+\Lambda u_{p}-nu_{f},\\
\tau&=J^TF.
\end{align}
$$
Thus the intrinsic set point is converted into a Cartesian set point, and the regulation law of FIC 1 is applied directly.

#### Tracking Controller

For a time-varying intrinsic reference, substitute the converted reference and its derivatives into the tracking law of FIC 1:
$$
\begin{align}
u_{p}&=T\ddot x_{t,d}
+k_v\left(T\dot x_{t,d}-\dot p\right)
+k_p\left(p_0+Tx_{t,d}-p\right),\\
u_{f}&=f_d+k_fe_f,\qquad e_f=f_d-f_m,\\
F&=\eta+\Lambda u_{p}-nu_{f},\\
\tau&=J^TF.
\end{align}
$$
Equivalently, $u_{p}=\ddot p_d+k_v\dot e_p+k_pe_p$. The $\eta$ term compensates the known robot dynamics, including gravity in the vertical plane. In both realizations, $k_f=0$ gives the feedforward normal-force command $u_{f}=f_d$.

Both acceleration commands satisfy $n^Tu_{p}=0$ under maintained contact. Hence they realize the nominal controller with $u_{t}=T^Tu_{p}$ and $Tu_{t}=u_{p}$. For tracking, the corresponding scalar command is
$$
\begin{align}
u_{t}=\ddot x_{t,d}+k_v\dot e_t+k_pe_t.
\end{align}
$$
The regulator is the special case $\dot x_{t,d}=\ddot x_{t,d}=0$.

#### Closed-Loop Error Dynamics

Substitution into the nominal closed-loop equations yields
$$
\begin{align}
\ddot p&=T\ddot x_{t,d}+k_v\dot e_p+k_pe_p,\\
f&=f_d+k_f(f_d-f).
\end{align}
$$
Since $\ddot e_p=T\ddot x_{t,d}-\ddot p$, the error dynamics is
$$
\begin{align}
\ddot e_p+k_v\dot e_p+k_pe_p&=0,\\
\ddot e_t+k_v\dot e_t+k_pe_t&=0,\\
(1+k_f)e_f&=0.
\end{align}
$$
The scalar motion-error equation follows from $e_t=T^Te_p$ and the constant tangent $T$. Setting the intrinsic reference derivatives to zero gives the same error equations for regulation. Thus both realizations share the closed-loop error system of FIC 1.

#### Stability for Regulation and Tracking

The motion-error characteristic equation is
$$
\begin{align}
\mu^2+k_v\mu+k_p=0.
\end{align}
$$
Since $k_p>0$ and $k_v>0$, both roots have strictly negative real parts. Therefore the Cartesian and intrinsic motion errors converge exponentially to zero on the maintained-contact state space:
$$
\begin{align}
p(t)-\left(p_0+Tx_{t,d}(t)\right)&\to0,\\
\dot p(t)-T\dot x_{t,d}(t)&\to0,\\
x_t(t)-x_{t,d}(t)&\to0,\\
\dot x_t(t)-\dot x_{t,d}(t)&\to0.
\end{align}
$$
Since $1+k_f>0$, the algebraic force-error relation gives $f=f_d$ exactly in the ideal rigid-contact model. These conclusions hold while the trajectory remains in the nonsingular operating region and rigid contact is maintained. For a constant $x_{t,d}$, they reduce to regulation at $p_0+Tx_{t,d}$ with $\dot p\to0$. With the true geometry, the reference conversion is exact and gives the same Cartesian motion and force commands as FIC 1 supplied with the converted reference and the same gains.

### FIC 3: Intrinsic Coordinate Tracking

This full-information controller uses the intrinsic reference $x_{t,d}$ and the scalar intrinsic motion error $e_t$ directly to generate the tangential acceleration command $u_{t}$. The true geometry $n$, $T=T(n)$, and the fixed contact point $p_0$ are known. The desired normal force is the constant $f_d>0$, and the frictionless rigid unilateral contact and exact scalar force measurement $f_m=f$ specified in the Scope are assumed throughout. Choose scalar gains $k_p>0$, $k_v>0$, and $k_f\geq0$ for both controller realizations below.

#### Intrinsic Error Signals

Since the contact line is fixed, $T$ is constant. The actual intrinsic position and velocity are computed from the Cartesian measurements as
$$
\begin{align}
x_t&=T^T(p-p_0),\\
\dot x_t&=T^T\dot p.
\end{align}
$$
For a twice continuously differentiable intrinsic reference $x_{t,d}(t)$ with known first and second derivatives, define
$$
\begin{align}
e_t&=x_{t,d}-x_t,\\
\dot e_t&=\dot x_{t,d}-\dot x_t.
\end{align}
$$
The controller evaluates these scalar errors directly. A Cartesian desired position $p_d$ and Cartesian motion error $e_p$ are not required for implementation.

#### Regulator

For a constant intrinsic reference $x_{t,d}$, $\dot x_{t,d}=\ddot x_{t,d}=0$ and $\dot e_t=-\dot x_t$. Define
$$
\begin{align}
u_{t}&=k_pe_t-k_v\dot x_t,\\
u_{f}&=f_d+k_fe_f,\qquad e_f=f_d-f_m,\\
F&=\eta+\Lambda T\left(k_pe_t-k_v\dot x_t\right)-nu_{f},\\
\tau&=J^TF.
\end{align}
$$
The motion command is formed entirely from the intrinsic set point, position error, and velocity.

#### Tracking Controller

For a time-varying intrinsic reference, define
$$
\begin{align}
u_{t}&=\ddot x_{t,d}+k_v\dot e_t+k_pe_t,\\
u_{f}&=f_d+k_fe_f,\qquad e_f=f_d-f_m,\\
F&=\eta+\Lambda T\left(\ddot x_{t,d}+k_v\dot e_t+k_pe_t\right)-nu_{f},\\
\tau&=J^TF.
\end{align}
$$
The term $\ddot x_{t,d}$ provides intrinsic acceleration feedforward, and the remaining motion terms provide scalar error feedback. The $\eta$ term compensates the known robot dynamics, including gravity in the vertical plane. In both realizations, $k_f=0$ gives the feedforward normal-force command $u_{f}=f_d$.

The Cartesian acceleration command is obtained from the scalar control command:
$$
\begin{align}
u_{p}=Tu_{t},\qquad n^Tu_{p}=0.
\end{align}
$$
Thus both realizations have the nominal force law $F=\eta+\Lambda Tu_{t}-nu_{f}$. The regulator is the special case $\dot x_{t,d}=\ddot x_{t,d}=0$ of the intrinsic tracking controller.

#### Closed-Loop Error Dynamics

Substituting the tracking commands into the nominal closed-loop equations $\ddot x_t=u_{t}$ and $f=u_{f}$ gives
$$
\begin{align}
\ddot x_t&=\ddot x_{t,d}+k_v\dot e_t+k_pe_t,\\
f&=f_d+k_f(f_d-f).
\end{align}
$$
Using $\ddot e_t=\ddot x_{t,d}-\ddot x_t$ and rearranging yields
$$
\begin{align}
\ddot e_t+k_v\dot e_t+k_pe_t&=0,\\
(1+k_f)e_f&=0.
\end{align}
$$
For the regulator, $\dot e_t=-\dot x_t$ and $\ddot e_t=-\ddot x_t$ give the same error equations. Both controller realizations therefore share the same scalar closed-loop motion-error system and algebraic force-error relation.

#### Stability for Regulation and Tracking

The intrinsic motion-error characteristic equation is
$$
\begin{align}
\mu^2+k_v\mu+k_p=0.
\end{align}
$$
Since $k_p>0$ and $k_v>0$, both roots have strictly negative real parts. Hence $(e_t,\dot e_t)=(0,0)$ is exponentially stable, and
$$
\begin{align}
x_t(t)-x_{t,d}(t)&\to0,\\
\dot x_t(t)-\dot x_{t,d}(t)&\to0.
\end{align}
$$
Under maintained contact, $p=p_0+Tx_t$ and $\dot p=T\dot x_t$, so the same result gives exponential convergence to the corresponding Cartesian motion $p_0+Tx_{t,d}$ and velocity $T\dot x_{t,d}$. Since $1+k_f>0$, the algebraic force-error relation gives $f=f_d$ exactly in the ideal rigid-contact model. These conclusions hold while the trajectory remains in the nonsingular operating region and rigid contact is maintained. For a constant intrinsic set point, they reduce to position regulation with $\dot x_t\to0$ and $\dot p\to0$.

With the true geometry and the same gains, $e_p=Te_t$ and $\dot e_p=T\dot e_t$ make the resulting Cartesian acceleration and force commands identical to those of FIC 2. Here the controller forms $u_{t}$ from the intrinsic errors and maps the resulting acceleration command through $T$.

### FIC 4: Intrinsic Coordinate Tracking with Task Space Reference

This full-information controller receives a task-space reference $p_d$ and converts it into the intrinsic reference $x_{t,d}$ before applying the scalar-error control law of FIC 3. The true geometry $n$, $T=T(n)$, and the fixed contact point $p_0$ are known. The desired normal force is the constant $f_d>0$, and the frictionless rigid unilateral contact and exact scalar force measurement $f_m=f$ specified in the Scope are assumed throughout. Choose scalar gains $k_p>0$, $k_v>0$, and $k_f\geq0$ for both controller realizations below.

#### Reference Conversion and Intrinsic Errors

For a twice continuously differentiable task-space reference with known derivatives, define
$$
\begin{align}
x_{t,d}&=T^T(p_d-p_0),\\
\dot x_{t,d}&=T^T\dot p_d,\\
\ddot x_{t,d}&=T^T\ddot p_d.
\end{align}
$$
The derivative expressions follow because $T$ and $p_0$ are constant. With $x_t=T^T(p-p_0)$ and $\dot x_t=T^T\dot p$, the intrinsic errors are
$$
\begin{align}
e_t&=x_{t,d}-x_t=T^T(p_d-p),\\
\dot e_t&=\dot x_{t,d}-\dot x_t=T^T(\dot p_d-\dot p).
\end{align}
$$
The Cartesian motion represented by the converted reference is
$$
\begin{align}
p_{d,t}=p_0+Tx_{t,d}=p_0+Q(p_d-p_0).
\end{align}
$$
For a reference on the true contact line, as specified in the Reference Architecture, $p_{d,t}=p_d$. If a supplied reference has a normal offset, the conversion retains its tangential coordinate and specifies its projection $p_{d,t}$ on the contact line. The intrinsic error then describes tracking of that projected reference.

#### Regulator

For a constant task-space reference $p_d$, the converted $x_{t,d}$ is constant. Applying the intrinsic regulation law gives
$$
\begin{align}
u_{t}&=k_pe_t-k_v\dot x_t
=k_pT^T(p_d-p)-k_vT^T\dot p,\\
u_{f}&=f_d+k_fe_f,\qquad e_f=f_d-f_m,\\
F&=\eta+\Lambda Tu_{t}-nu_{f},\\
\tau&=J^TF.
\end{align}
$$
Thus the task-space set point is converted into an intrinsic set point, and the controller evaluates scalar tangential errors.

#### Tracking Controller

For a time-varying task-space reference, applying the intrinsic tracking law gives
$$
\begin{align}
u_{t}&=\ddot x_{t,d}+k_v\dot e_t+k_pe_t\\
&=T^T\ddot p_d+k_vT^T(\dot p_d-\dot p)+k_pT^T(p_d-p),\\
u_{f}&=f_d+k_fe_f,\qquad e_f=f_d-f_m,\\
F&=\eta+\Lambda Tu_{t}-nu_{f},\\
\tau&=J^TF.
\end{align}
$$
The $\eta$ term compensates the known robot dynamics, including gravity in the vertical plane. Both realizations generate a tangential Cartesian acceleration command $u_{p}=Tu_{t}$, with $n^Tu_{p}=0$. The choice $k_f=0$ gives the feedforward normal-force command $u_{f}=f_d$, and the regulator is the special case $\dot p_d=\ddot p_d=0$.

#### Closed-Loop Error Dynamics

Substitution into the nominal closed-loop equations gives
$$
\begin{align}
\ddot x_t&=T^T\ddot p_d+k_v\dot e_t+k_pe_t,\\
f&=f_d+k_f(f_d-f).
\end{align}
$$
Using $\ddot e_t=T^T\ddot p_d-\ddot x_t$ yields
$$
\begin{align}
\ddot e_t+k_v\dot e_t+k_pe_t&=0,\\
(1+k_f)e_f&=0.
\end{align}
$$
For regulation, $\dot e_t=-\dot x_t$ and $\ddot e_t=-\ddot x_t$ give the same error equations. Thus both realizations share the scalar closed-loop error system of FIC 3.

#### Stability for Regulation and Tracking

The intrinsic motion-error characteristic equation is
$$
\begin{align}
\mu^2+k_v\mu+k_p=0.
\end{align}
$$
Since $k_p>0$ and $k_v>0$, both roots have strictly negative real parts. Hence $(e_t,\dot e_t)=(0,0)$ is exponentially stable, and the tangential tracking errors satisfy
$$
\begin{align}
T^T\left(p(t)-p_d(t)\right)&\to0,\\
T^T\left(\dot p(t)-\dot p_d(t)\right)&\to0.
\end{align}
$$
Under maintained contact, the corresponding Cartesian convergence is
$$
\begin{align}
p(t)-p_{d,t}(t)&\to0,\\
\dot p(t)-Q\dot p_d(t)&\to0.
\end{align}
$$
For a reference on the true contact line, these limits give full Cartesian position and velocity tracking. For a reference with a normal offset, the normal difference is determined by the contact constraint as $n^T(p-p_d)=-n^T(p_d-p_0)$; the achieved Cartesian target is its projection. Since $1+k_f>0$, the algebraic force-error relation gives $f=f_d$ exactly in the ideal rigid-contact model. These conclusions hold while the trajectory remains in the nonsingular operating region and rigid contact is maintained. For a constant $p_d$, they reduce to regulation at $p_{d,t}$ with $\dot p\to0$.

With an admissible task-space reference, the true geometry, and the same gains, $Tu_{t}=\ddot p_d+k_v\dot e_p+k_pe_p$. The resulting Cartesian acceleration and force commands are therefore identical to those of FIC 1; here the reference and feedback errors are first expressed in the intrinsic coordinate.

## Adaptive Control (AC)

### Uncertainty Parameterization

The candidates of uncertain parameters for adaptive control are listed as follows
- Unit normal, e.g. CCTA26 
- 2-D Surface inclination angle, e.g. [[ccta26u3]]
- General normal vector, e.g. [[KarDou2009]]
- Linear parameterized normal contact force error, e.g. [[DouKar2007]]
#### 2-D Surface Angle

Since the surface normal satisfies $n\in S^{1}$, the geometry estimate $\hat n$ should remain on the same unit circle. Choose a fixed unit reference vector $n_0\in S^{1}$. Define the planar rotation as
$$
\begin{align}
R_{2}(\theta)
=
\begin{bmatrix}
\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\
\sin\theta&\cos\theta
\end{bmatrix}
\in SO(2).
\end{align}
$$
The true and estimated normals are
$$
\begin{align}
n&=R_{2}(\theta)n_0, \qquad
\hat n=R_{2}(\hat\theta)n_0.
\end{align}
$$
For example, choose the upward unit vector $n_0=[0\;1]^T$ in $B$. Then
$$
\begin{align}
n&=\begin{bmatrix}-\sin\theta\\\cos\theta\end{bmatrix}, \qquad
\hat n=\begin{bmatrix}-\sin\hat\theta\\\cos\hat\theta\end{bmatrix}.
\end{align}
$$
Here $\theta=0$ corresponds to a horizontal contact line with an upward normal. With $T=R_{\text{-90}}n$, $\theta$ is the counterclockwise inclination of the tangent from the positive horizontal axis. 

The scalar angles are defined modulo $2\pi$. Since $R_{2}(\cdot)$ is orthogonal,
$$
\begin{align}
\|n\|=\|\hat n\|=1
\end{align}
$$
for all values of $\theta$ and $\hat\theta$. Consequently, an adaptive law may update the scalar parameter $\hat\theta$ without requiring an additional unit-norm constraint on $\hat n$. The estimated projector and tangent basis are
$$
\begin{align}
\hat Q&=I_2-\hat n\hat n^{T}, \\
\hat T&=T(\hat n)=R_{\text{-90}}\hat n\in\mathbb R^2.
\end{align}
$$
### Estimated Reference

#### Task-Space Reference Case

When a task-space reference is given, it may not satisfy the contact constraint, i.e. may not be on the contact surface. Taking such an infeasible reference may lead to excessive contact forces or contact loss, depending on the direction of the reference error and the control law. Therefore, the reference is projected onto the estimated contact surface as 
$$
\begin{align}
\hat p_{d} = p_{0} + \hat Q (p_{d}-p_{0}).
\end{align}
$$
Using this estimated reference, the estimated task-space error is 
$$
\begin{align}
\hat e_{p} & = \hat Q(\hat p_{d} - p) \\
 & =\hat Q(p_{0} + \hat Q (p_{d}-p_{0}))- \hat Qp \\
 & =\hat Q(p_{d}-p).
\end{align}
$$
Here $\hat Q^2=\hat Q$ is used. Thus the estimated error is the projection of the actual task-space error $e_p=p_d-p$ onto the estimated tangent subspace: $\hat e_p=\hat Qe_p$.

Consider a steady state with a constant reference $p_d$ and robot position $p_\infty$. Assume maintained contact, zero estimated projected error, and a nondegenerate estimated normal:
$$
\begin{align}
n^T(p_\infty-p_0)&=0,\\
\hat e_{p,\infty}=\hat Q(p_d-p_\infty)&=0,\\
n^T\hat n&\neq0.
\end{align}
$$
The original reference is achieved when $p_\infty=p_d$. Its true tangential position target is the point on the contact line with the same true tangential coordinate:
$$
\begin{align}
p_{d,t}&=p_0+Q(p_d-p_0),\\
x_{t,d}&=T^T(p_d-p_0).
\end{align}
$$
Achieving this tangential target means $T^T(p_d-p_\infty)=0$, equivalently $p_\infty=p_{d,t}$ under maintained contact. Here the tangential component is taken relative to the true contact line, whereas $\hat e_{p,\infty}=0$ concerns the estimated tangent.

Define the signed normal distance $d_{p_d}=n^T(p_d-p_0)$, positive on the side toward which $n$ points. Since $\hat Q(p_d-p_\infty)=0$, the position difference lies along $\hat n$. Taking its component along $n$ gives
$$
\begin{align}
p_d-p_\infty&=\frac{d_{p_d}}{n^T\hat n}\hat n, \qquad
p_\infty=p_d-\frac{d_{p_d}}{n^T\hat n}\hat n.
\end{align}
$$
Consequently, the true tangential error is
$$
\begin{align}
e_{t,\infty}
=T^T(p_d-p_\infty)
=\frac{d_{p_d}\,T^T\hat n}{n^T\hat n}.
\end{align}
$$
If $d_{p_d}=0$, the reference lies on the true contact line, $p_\infty=p_d=p_{d,t}$, $e_{t,\infty}=0.$ Both the original reference and its tangential target are achieved, even if the steady-state geometry estimate is biased, provided $n^T\hat n\neq0$.

If $d_{p_d}\neq0$, the original reference cannot be achieved under maintained rigid contact. Its tangential target is achieved if and only if $T^T\hat n=0$, i.e. $\hat Q=Q$, or $\hat n=\pm n$. Otherwise, $e_{t,\infty}\neq0$: zero estimated projected error does not imply zero true tangential error. The resulting position is an oblique projection along $\hat n$. 

With exact steady-state parameter identification, $\hat n=n$ and $\hat Q=Q$. Therefore, $p_\infty=p_d-d_{p_d}n=p_{d,t}$, $e_{t,\infty}=0.$ The true tangential target is always achieved. The original $p_d$ is achieved only if it lies on the true contact line; otherwise, its normal offset remains infeasible. In this case, $\hat p_d=p_\infty=p_{d,t}$.

## I&I Adaptive Control (IAC)

Instead of using the raw parameter estimate $\hat\theta$, I&I adaptive control introduces an additional  estimate correction term $\beta(x_{syst})$, where $x_{syst}$ refers to the system states, and is omitted when writing $\beta$ for simplicity. We therefore define the I&I effective estimate 
$$
\begin{align}
\bar\theta=\hat\theta+\beta.
\end{align}
$$
The corresponding estimate of $n$ is
$$
\begin{align}
\bar n=R_{2}(\bar\theta)n_0=R_{2}(\beta)\hat n.
\end{align}
$$
Define the I&I parameter estimate error
$$
\begin{align}
z
=
\bar\theta-\theta
=
\hat\theta-\theta+\beta,
\end{align}
$$
then 
$$
\begin{align}
\bar n=R_{2}(z)n.
\end{align}
$$
We note that corresponding to $z=0$, is nominally defined as 
$$
\begin{align}
\mathcal M=\{(x_{syst},\hat\theta):\hat\theta-\theta+\beta=0\}.
\end{align}
$$
An successful I&I adaptive control design constructs an invariant and attractive $\mathcal M$, which means (i) if $z(0)=0$, then $z(t)\equiv{0}$; (ii) if $z(0)\neq{0}$ , then $z(t)\to{0}$. Hence on this manifold, $\bar n=n$, and the IAC reduces to the FIC.

With $T=R_{\text{-90}}n$ and $\bar T=T(\bar n)=R_{\text{-90}}\bar n$, the 2-D rotation geometry gives
$$
\begin{align}
\bar n&=\cos z\,n-\sin z\,T,\\
\bar T&=\sin z\,n+\cos z\,T.
\end{align}
$$
Hence
$$
\begin{align}
n^T\bar n&=\cos z,\\
T^T\bar T&=\cos z,\\
T^T\bar n&=-\sin z,\\
n^T\bar T&=\sin z.
\end{align}
$$

### IAC 1: Task-Space Control, Uncertain Surface Angle

This design takes FIC 1 with $k_f=0$ as the target system. The reference $p_d(t)$ lies on the true fixed contact line, and $p_d$, $\dot p_d$, and $\ddot p_d$ are known and bounded. Regulation is obtained by setting $\dot p_d=\ddot p_d=0$. The robot model is known, the constant surface angle $\theta$ is unknown, and frictionless rigid unilateral contact is already established at initialization, with $\phi(p(0))=0$ and $n^T\dot p(0)=0$.

As in FIC 1, the scalar normal contact force $f\geq0$ is measured exactly, with $f_m=f$, and the update law below uses $f$ directly. The construction gives a local angular domain on which the closed loop guarantees a strictly positive compressive reaction, maintains unilateral contact, and achieves exponential parameter convergence. Contact establishment, impacts, and contact-mode transitions are excluded. No force-vector measurement, acceleration measurement, or numerical differentiation of the measured force is required.

#### Target System

Use the Cartesian acceleration command of FIC 1 directly:
$$
\begin{align}
u_p&=\ddot p_d+k_v\dot e_p+k_pe_p,\\
e_p&=p_d-p,\qquad \dot e_p=\dot p_d-\dot p,\\
k_p&>0,\qquad k_v>0.
\end{align}
$$
Because the reference and actual motion lie on the true contact line, $n^Tu_p=0$, even though $n$ is unknown. Thus $u_p$ can be computed without estimating a projector or differentiating an estimated reference. The target dynamics are exactly those of FIC 1:
$$
\begin{align}
\ddot e_p+k_v\dot e_p+k_pe_p&=0,\\
f&=f_d,\qquad f_d>0.
\end{align}
$$

#### Explicit Momentum Correction and Controller

Choose a known nominal angle $\theta_c$ and define fixed directions
$$
\begin{align}
n_c&=R_2(\theta_c)n_0,\\
T_c&=R_{\text{-90}}n_c.
\end{align}
$$
The subscript $c$ denotes the chosen angular center, rather than the controller-initialization time $t_c$.

Define the measurable Cartesian momentum and its known drift:
$$
\begin{align}
m_p&=\Lambda(q)\dot p,\\
g_p&=F-\eta+\dot\Lambda(q,\dot q)\dot p,\\
\dot m_p&=g_p+nf.
\end{align}
$$
Here $\dot\Lambda=\sum_{i=1}^{2}(\partial\Lambda/\partial q_i)\dot q_i$ is computed from the known model and measured joint position and velocity. Ideal actuation is assumed: actuator dynamics, communication delays, saturation, and torque-tracking errors are neglected, so the actual applied joint torque satisfies $\tau_{\mathrm{act}}=\tau_{\mathrm{cmd}}=J^TF$. Consequently, the current force command $F$ is used directly to evaluate $g_p$.

Choose $\gamma>0$ and the explicit I&I correction
$$
\begin{align}
\beta(q,\dot q)&=-\gamma T_c^Tm_p,\\
\bar\theta&=\hat\theta+\beta,\\
\bar n&=R_2(\bar\theta)n_0.
\end{align}
$$
Use the following force law:
$$
\begin{align}
F&=\eta+\Lambda u_p-\bar n f_d,\\
\tau&=J^TF.
\end{align}
$$
This force law retains the Cartesian acceleration command $u_p$ and dynamic compensation of FIC 1, sets $k_f=0$ so that $u_f=f_d$, and replaces the true normal $n$ by the effective estimate $\bar n$. When $z=0$, $\bar n=n$ and the force law coincides exactly with FIC 1. The choice $k_f=0$ keeps the normal-force command constant and simplifies the convergence analysis; it is not required by the I&I method. In the ideal rigid-contact model, $\bar n=n$ already gives $f=f_d$ with this feedforward force command. The force-command channel therefore uses feedforward, while the measured force $f$ still enters the adaptive update and adjusts the commanded force direction through $\bar n$.

Update the internal estimate according to
$$
\begin{align}
\dot{\hat\theta}
&=\gamma T_c^T\left(g_p+\bar n f\right).
\end{align}
$$
The correction uses the fixed vector $T_c$, so the effective angle is explicit. No implicit angle inversion or unknown effective angular velocity is needed. Initialize the internal coordinate using the chosen effective angle:
$$
\begin{align}
\hat\theta(0)
=\bar\theta(0)+\gamma T_c^T\Lambda(q(0))\dot p(0).
\end{align}
$$
For regulation, replace $u_p$ by $k_pe_p-k_v\dot p$; the correction and update law are unchanged. All implemented expressions use the known robot model, the reference, $q$, $\dot q$, the measured scalar force $f$, and the internal state $\hat\theta$.

#### Actual Closed-Loop Error Dynamics

On the active-contact branch, substituting the force law into the true dynamics gives
$$
\begin{align}
\Lambda(\ddot p-u_p)&=nf-\bar n f_d.
\end{align}
$$

##### Angle-Estimation Error

For $z=\bar\theta-\theta$, differentiate the actual correction along the momentum equation and substitute the implemented update:
$$
\begin{align}
\dot z
&=\dot{\hat\theta}-\gamma T_c^T\dot m_p\\
&=\gamma f\,T_c^T(\bar n-n)\\
&=-\gamma f\left[
\sin(\bar\theta-\theta_c)-\sin(\theta-\theta_c)
\right].
\end{align}
$$
This equation is derived from the implemented update and the true plant. It does not require knowledge of $z$ or $n$. Define the averaged slope of the sine map $\vartheta\mapsto\sin(\vartheta-\theta_c)$ as
$$
\begin{align}
H(\bar\theta,\theta)
&=\int_0^1\cos(\theta-\theta_c+s z)\,ds.
\end{align}
$$
The fundamental theorem of calculus gives
$$
\begin{align}
\sin(\bar\theta-\theta_c)-\sin(\theta-\theta_c)&=H(\bar\theta,\theta)z.
\end{align}
$$
Hence
$$
\begin{align}
\dot z&=-\gamma fH(\bar\theta,\theta)z.
\end{align}
$$
These identities hold before imposing an angular-domain or force-sign restriction.

##### Motion Error

Under maintained contact, $\ddot p$ is tangential. The command $u_p$ is also tangential because $n^Tu_p=0$. Thus
$$
\begin{align}
\ddot p&=T\ddot x_t,\\
u_p&=TT^Tu_p=T(T^Tu_p).
\end{align}
$$
Left-multiplying the actual closed-loop equation $\Lambda(\ddot p-u_p)=nf-\bar n f_d$ by $T^T$ gives
$$
\begin{align}
T^T\Lambda(\ddot p-u_p)
&=T^Tnf-T^T\bar n f_d.
\end{align}
$$
Substitute the tangential representations above and use $T^Tn=0$, $T^T\bar n=-\sin z$, and $\Lambda_t=T^T\Lambda T$:
$$
\begin{align}
T^T\Lambda T\left(\ddot x_t-T^Tu_p\right)
&=0-(-\sin z)f_d,\\
\Lambda_t\left(\ddot x_t-T^Tu_p\right)
&=f_d\sin z.
\end{align}
$$
The true normal contact force $nf$ contributes no tangential component, whereas the estimated force direction contributes through $T^T\bar n=-\sin z$. Dividing by $\Lambda_t>0$ yields
$$
\begin{align}
\ddot x_t&=T^Tu_p+\frac{f_d}{\Lambda_t}\sin z.
\end{align}
$$
Substituting $u_p=\ddot p_d+k_v\dot e_p+k_pe_p$ and using $T^Tu_p=\ddot x_{t,d}+k_v\dot e_t+k_pe_t$ together with $e_t=x_{t,d}-x_t$ yields
$$
\begin{align}
\ddot e_t+k_v\dot e_t+k_pe_t
&=-\frac{f_d}{\Lambda_t}\sin z.
\end{align}
$$
The effective parameter error $z=\bar\theta-\theta$ therefore enters the motion-error system as the disturbance $-f_d\sin z/\Lambda_t$. Since $k_p,k_v>0$ and $\Lambda_t\geq\underline m>0$, the nominal motion-error dynamics are exponentially stable and this disturbance is bounded even before the parameter estimate converges. Convergence of the effective estimate, $\bar\theta\to\theta$, makes the disturbance vanish and is sufficient for $e_t,\dot e_t\to0$, hence for Cartesian motion tracking under maintained contact.

Parameter mismatch can otherwise leave a tracking bias: for constant $\Lambda_t$ and a residual angle error $z_*$, the steady motion error is $e_{t,\infty}=-f_d\sin z_*/(k_p\Lambda_t)$. If the parameter error instead converges exponentially to zero, the motion errors also converge exponentially, with a decay rate governed by both the parameter convergence and the nominal motion dynamics. Thus the motion loop remains stable during adaptation, while parameter convergence removes the geometry-induced forcing that can prevent exact tracking.

##### Contact Force

Since $n^T\ddot p=n^Tu_p=0$, the rigid-contact reaction satisfies
$$
\begin{align}
f&=\frac{n^T\Lambda^{-1}\bar n}{n^T\Lambda^{-1}n}f_d.
\end{align}
$$
Define the geometry-dependent force factor by
$$
\begin{align}
\rho(q,z)
&=\frac{n^T\Lambda^{-1}\bar n}{n^T\Lambda^{-1}n}.
\end{align}
$$
Substituting $\bar n=\cos z\,n-\sin z\,T$ gives
$$
\begin{align}
\rho(q,z)
&=\frac{n^T\Lambda^{-1}(\cos z\,n-\sin z\,T)}
{n^T\Lambda^{-1}n}\\
&=\cos z-\frac{n^T\Lambda^{-1}T}{n^T\Lambda^{-1}n}\sin z.
\end{align}
$$
To express the coefficient using $\Lambda$ rather than $\Lambda^{-1}$, write the inertia in the orthonormal contact frame $R_s=[T\ n]$:
$$
\begin{align}
R_s^T\Lambda R_s
&=\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\b&c\end{bmatrix},\\
a&=T^T\Lambda T=\Lambda_t,\qquad
b=T^T\Lambda n,\qquad c=n^T\Lambda n,\\
R_s^T\Lambda^{-1}R_s
&=(R_s^T\Lambda R_s)^{-1}
=\frac{1}{ac-b^2}
\begin{bmatrix}c&-b\\-b&a\end{bmatrix}.
\end{align}
$$
The numerator and denominator in the coefficient of $\sin z$ are $n^T\Lambda^{-1}T$ and $n^T\Lambda^{-1}n$. Since the columns of $R_s$ are $T$ and $n$, expanding the transformed matrix shows where these two quantities appear:
$$
\begin{align}
R_s^T\Lambda^{-1}R_s
&=\begin{bmatrix}T^T\\n^T\end{bmatrix}
\Lambda^{-1}
\begin{bmatrix}T&n\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}
T^T\Lambda^{-1}T&T^T\Lambda^{-1}n\\
n^T\Lambda^{-1}T&n^T\Lambda^{-1}n
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{align}
$$
Comparing its $(2,1)$ and $(2,2)$ entries with the explicit inverse above yields
$$
\begin{align}
n^T\Lambda^{-1}T&=-\frac{b}{ac-b^2},\\
n^T\Lambda^{-1}n&=\frac{a}{ac-b^2}.
\end{align}
$$
Substituting these expressions into the coefficient of $\sin z$, and using $a=\Lambda_t$ and $b=T^T\Lambda n$, gives
$$
\begin{align}
-\frac{n^T\Lambda^{-1}T}{n^T\Lambda^{-1}n}
&=-\frac{-b/(ac-b^2)}{a/(ac-b^2)}
=\frac{b}{a}
=\frac{T^T\Lambda n}{\Lambda_t}
\end{align}
$$
We define the normalized tangential-normal inertia coupling term as
$$
\begin{align}
r_\Lambda(q)=\frac{T^T\Lambda n}{\Lambda_t}.
\end{align}
$$
Consequently,
$$
\begin{align}
\rho(q,z)&=\cos z+r_\Lambda(q)\sin z.
\end{align}
$$
Thus the actual contact force satisfies $f=\rho(q,z)f_d$.

#### Positive Contact Force Domain

Since $f=\rho(q,z)f_d$ and $f_d>0$, a positive lower bound on $\rho$ guarantees positive contact force. We first bound the normalized tangential–normal inertia coupling $r_\Lambda=T^T\Lambda n/\Lambda_t$, then select an angular sector and prove that the implemented update preserves it. This combines force positivity and parameter convergence in one domain argument.

We first determine the inertia coupling bound. For the planar arm, the eigenvalues of $\Lambda(q)$ are the effective masses along its inertia principal directions. Their uniform bounds $0<\underline m\leq\lambda_1(q)\leq\lambda_2(q)\leq\overline m$ give a configuration-independent limit on the normalized tangential–normal coupling $|r_\Lambda(q)|$. Define the uniform inertia coupling bound $\chi_\Lambda$ by
$$
\begin{align}
\chi_\Lambda
&=\frac{\overline m-\underline m}{2\sqrt{\overline m\,\underline m}}.
\end{align}
$$
Our goal is to prove that this quantity bounds the normalized inertia coupling throughout the operating region:
$$
\begin{align}
|r_\Lambda(q)|&\leq\chi_\Lambda.
\end{align}
$$

To establish this bound, use the contact-frame inertia $R_s^T\Lambda R_s=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\b&c\end{smallmatrix}\right]$, where $a=\Lambda_t>0$ and $r_\Lambda=b/a$. The orthogonal change of coordinates preserves the inertia eigenvalues, denoted by $0<\lambda_1\leq\lambda_2$. The trace and determinant therefore satisfy
$$
\begin{align}
a+c&=\lambda_1+\lambda_2,\\
ac-b^2&=\lambda_1\lambda_2.
\end{align}
$$
Eliminating $c$ expresses the coupling entry $b$ in terms of $a$ and the eigenvalues:
$$
\begin{align}
b^2
&=a(\lambda_1+\lambda_2-a)-\lambda_1\lambda_2\\
&=(a-\lambda_1)(\lambda_2-a).
\end{align}
$$
Divide by $a^2$ and complete the square in $1/a$:
$$
\begin{align}
\frac{b^2}{a^2}
&=\frac{\lambda_1+\lambda_2}{a}-1
-\frac{\lambda_1\lambda_2}{a^2}\\
&=\frac{(\lambda_2-\lambda_1)^2}{4\lambda_1\lambda_2}
-\lambda_1\lambda_2\left(
\frac{1}{a}-\frac{\lambda_1+\lambda_2}{2\lambda_1\lambda_2}
\right)^2\\
&\leq\frac{(\lambda_2-\lambda_1)^2}{4\lambda_1\lambda_2}.
\end{align}
$$
Taking square roots gives the coupling bound for this inertia. To make it uniform over the robot operating region, use $\underline m\leq\lambda_1\leq\lambda_2\leq\overline m$:
$$
\begin{align}
|r_\Lambda|
&=\frac{|b|}{a}
\leq\frac{\lambda_2-\lambda_1}{2\sqrt{\lambda_1\lambda_2}}\\
&=\frac12\left(
\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1}}
-\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_2}}
\right)\\
&\leq\frac12\left(
\sqrt{\frac{\overline m}{\underline m}}
-\sqrt{\frac{\underline m}{\overline m}}
\right)
=\chi_\Lambda.
\end{align}
$$

We then determine the angular sector. We work with continuous real lifts of the angles. Assume the true angle belongs to a known sector and initialize the effective estimate in that sector:
$$
\begin{align}
\theta,\ \bar\theta(0)&\in\mathcal I_c
=[\theta_c-\Delta,\theta_c+\Delta],\\
0&<\Delta<\frac{\pi}{4}.
\end{align}
$$

If both $\theta$ and $\bar\theta$ belong to $\mathcal I_c$, their difference satisfies
$$
\begin{align}
|z|&=|\bar\theta-\theta|
\leq|\bar\theta-\theta_c|+|\theta-\theta_c|
\leq2\Delta<\frac{\pi}{2}.
\end{align}
$$
Over the chosen angular sector, define the uniform lower bound of the force factor $\rho$ 
$$
\begin{align}
\rho_{\min}&=\cos(2\Delta)-\chi_\Lambda\sin(2\Delta).
\end{align}
$$
If $\rho_{\min}>0$, the chosen angular sector is admissible for guaranteeing positive contact force. This sufficient condition is checked before operation using the known angular-sector width and inertia bounds; no online evaluation of the unknown error $z=\bar\theta-\theta$ is required. Moreover, although a sufficiently small positive $\Delta$ makes $\rho_{\min}>0$, the chosen sector must also cover the full range of $\theta$ allowed by the prior information. 

For $|z|\leq2\Delta<\pi/2$, $\cos z=\cos|z|\geq\cos(2\Delta)$ and $|\sin z|\leq\sin(2\Delta)$. Combining these angular bounds with the uniform coupling bound yields
$$
\begin{align}
\rho(q,z)
&=\cos z+r_\Lambda(q)\sin z\\
&\geq\cos z-|r_\Lambda(q)|\,|\sin z|\\
&\geq\cos(2\Delta)-\chi_\Lambda\sin(2\Delta)
=\rho_{\min}>0.
\end{align}
$$
The last strict inequality is the prescribed sector-width condition. Multiplying by $f_d>0$ then gives the positive lower bound on the actual force through $f=\rho(q,z)f_d$.

Consequently, whenever $\theta,\bar\theta\in\mathcal I_c$,
$$
\begin{align}
|z|&\leq2\Delta,\\
\rho(q,z)&\geq\rho_{\min}>0,\\
f&\geq\rho_{\min}f_d>0.
\end{align}
$$
A numerical example of the positive contact force domain is provided in [[#IAC 1 Supplement: Numerical Example of Positive Contact Force Domain|the appendix supplement for IAC 1]].

#### Invariance and Exponential Parameter Convergence

While $\theta,\bar\theta\in\mathcal I_c$, the identity $z=\bar\theta-\theta$ gives
$$
\begin{align}
\theta+s z=(1-s)\theta+s\bar\theta\in[\theta_c-\Delta,\theta_c+\Delta]
\end{align}
$$
for every $s\in[0,1]$. Thus $|\theta-\theta_c+s z|\leq\Delta$, and the integrand defining $H$ satisfies $\cos\Delta\leq\cos(\theta-\theta_c+s z)\leq1$. Hence
$$
\begin{align}
\cos\Delta\leq H\leq1,\qquad
\dot z=-\gamma f H z.
\end{align}
$$
Since the true angle $\theta$ is constant, $\dot{\bar\theta}=\dot z$. Together with $f\geq\rho_{\min}f_d>0$ in the sector, the error equation gives
$$
\begin{align}
\dot{\bar\theta}&=-\gamma fH(\bar\theta-\theta),\\
\gamma fH&\geq\gamma\rho_{\min}f_d\cos\Delta>0.
\end{align}
$$
If $\bar\theta>\theta$, then $\dot{\bar\theta}<0$ and the estimate decreases; if $\bar\theta<\theta$, then $\dot{\bar\theta}>0$ and the estimate increases. When $\bar\theta=\theta$, its derivative is zero. Thus $\bar\theta$ moves toward the constant true angle and remains there upon reaching it.

The same sign argument applies at the sector boundaries because the true angle satisfies $\theta_c-\Delta\leq\theta\leq\theta_c+\Delta$. At the upper boundary,
$$
\begin{align}
\bar\theta=\theta_c+\Delta
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\dot{\bar\theta}
&=-\gamma fH(\theta_c+\Delta-\theta)\leq0.
\end{align}
$$
Thus the estimate can only decrease or remain at that boundary. At the lower boundary,
$$
\begin{align}
\bar\theta=\theta_c-\Delta
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\dot{\bar\theta}
&=-\gamma fH(\theta_c-\Delta-\theta)\geq0.
\end{align}
$$
Thus the estimate can only increase or remain at that boundary. In either case, the derivative is zero precisely when the true angle coincides with the boundary; otherwise it points into the sector.

Starting from $\bar\theta(0)\in\mathcal I_c$, the estimate therefore cannot exit the sector during active-contact evolution and stays between $\bar\theta(0)$ and $\theta$. To verify that unilateral contact itself persists, evaluate the normal acceleration that the same command would produce with the reaction set to zero:
$$
\begin{align}
a_{n,\mathrm{free}}
&=n^T\Lambda^{-1}(F-\eta)\\
&=-\bigl(n^T\Lambda^{-1}n\bigr)\rho(q,z)f_d<0.
\end{align}
$$
At zero gap and zero normal velocity, this acceleration would penetrate the surface, violating $\phi(p)\geq0$. The admissible reaction is instead $f=\rho(q,z)f_d\geq\rho_{\min}f_d>0$, which enforces $n^T\ddot p=0$ and keeps $\phi(p)=0$. The reaction has a strictly positive margin, so the active-contact branch cannot end through vanishing contact force. Together with the inward-pointing angular dynamics, this rules out either sector exit or contact loss as a first event. By continuation, $\mathcal I_c$ is forward invariant and unilateral contact is maintained while the trajectory remains in the nonsingular robot operating region. This argument certifies operation in the existing contact mode and introduces no contact-establishment, impact, or mode-transition dynamics.

For $V_z=z^2/2$, this gives
$$
\begin{align}
\dot V_z
&=-\gamma f H z^2
\leq-2\alpha_z V_z,\\
\alpha_z&=\gamma\rho_{\min}f_d\cos\Delta>0,\\
|z(t)|&\leq |z(0)|e^{-\alpha_z t}.
\end{align}
$$
The manifold $\mathcal M=\{\hat\theta-\theta-\gamma T_c^Tm_p=0\}$ is invariant and exponentially attractive in this domain. No motion persistence of excitation is required: the certified nonzero contact force supplies the parameter-error damping, including during regulation at rest.

The target-system immersion and its dynamic-matching equation are detailed in [[#IAC 1 Supplement: Immersion of the Target System|the appendix supplement for IAC 1]].

#### Motion and Force Tracking

The disturbance $-f_d\sin z/\Lambda_t$ in the motion-error equation decays exponentially because $\Lambda_t\geq\underline m$ and the angle error obeys the established exponential bound:
$$
\begin{align}
\left|\frac{f_d}{\Lambda_t}\sin z(t)\right|
&\leq\frac{f_d}{\underline m}|z(0)|e^{-\alpha_z t}.
\end{align}
$$
The motion-error system is a stable linear system driven by this exponentially decaying input. Consequently, $e_t$ and $\dot e_t$ converge exponentially to zero, with a rate that may be chosen below both $\alpha_z$ and the nominal motion decay rate. Since $e_p=Te_t$, the Cartesian errors converge as well.

The force error satisfies
$$
\begin{align}
f-f_d&=f_d\left[\cos z-1+r_\Lambda(q)\sin z\right],\\
|f-f_d|
&\leq f_d\left(\frac{z^2}{2}+\chi_\Lambda|z|\right).
\end{align}
$$
Thus $f\to f_d$ exponentially. Exact force matching holds on $\mathcal M$; away from it there is a transient force error. For regulation, $\dot p\to0$ implies $\beta\to0$ and $\hat\theta\to\theta$. During persistent tracking, it is the effective estimate $\bar\theta$ that converges to $\theta$; the raw internal estimate need not do so because $\beta$ need not vanish.

##### Scope

With bounded reference signals, the error estimates also give bounded $p$ and $\dot p$. On a compact smooth nonsingular robot operating region, $m_p$, $g_p$, $\beta$, $\hat\theta$, and the commanded input are bounded. As with FIC 1, the robot conclusions apply while the trajectory remains in that operating region; the angular-sector proof does not certify avoidance of a manipulator singularity.

The sufficient conditions for this construction are therefore an admissible task-space reference, initially established unilateral contact with zero normal velocity, the known robot model and inertia bounds, exact scalar force measurement as in FIC 1, and the stated angular-sector initialization. Within that domain, the strict reaction-force bound proves contact maintenance as well as parameter convergence. Outside the certified angular domain, the constrained reaction formula may fail to yield a strictly positive force, and continued contact and convergence are not guaranteed. Contact establishment, impacts, and contact-mode transitions are outside the analysis.

#### Main Theorem

**Theorem (Exponential motion-force tracking under unilateral contact).** Consider the two-DoF manipulator and the IAC 1 controller defined above. Suppose that the following conditions hold:

1. The robot kinematics and dynamics are exactly known and smooth on a neighborhood of a compact operating region $\mathcal Q$. The Jacobian is nonsingular there, $\underline m I_2\preceq\Lambda(q)\preceq\overline m I_2$ with $0<\underline m\leq\overline m$, and the configuration remains in $\mathcal Q$. Joint position, joint velocity, and the scalar normal force are measured exactly, and the ideal-actuation assumption holds.
2. The contact surface is fixed and frictionless, with $n=R_2(\theta)n_0$ for a constant unknown $\theta$. Unilateral contact is initially established with $\phi(p(0))=0$ and $n^T\dot p(0)=0$.
3. The reference satisfies $p_d\in C^2([0,\infty);\mathcal S)$, the signals $p_d,\dot p_d,\ddot p_d$ are bounded, and the desired force $f_d>0$ is constant.
4. For a known center $\theta_c$, the true angle and the initial effective estimate satisfy
$$
\begin{align}
\theta,\bar\theta(0)&\in\mathcal I_c
=[\theta_c-\Delta,\theta_c+\Delta],
\qquad 0<\Delta<\frac{\pi}{4},\\
\chi_\Lambda&=\frac{\overline m-\underline m}
{2\sqrt{\overline m\,\underline m}},\\
\rho_{\min}&=\cos(2\Delta)-\chi_\Lambda\sin(2\Delta)>0.
\end{align}
$$
Choose $k_p,k_v,\gamma>0$, set $k_f=0$, and apply the following torque command and parameter update, written directly in terms of the measured signals and the known robot model:
$$
\begin{align}
\tau
&=C(q,\dot q)\dot q+G(q)\\
&\quad+M(q)J^{-1}(q)\Bigl[
\ddot p_d-\dot J(q,\dot q)\dot q
+k_v\bigl(\dot p_d-J(q)\dot q\bigr)
+k_p\bigl(p_d-h(q)\bigr)
\Bigr]\\
&\quad-J^T(q)R_2\!\left(
\hat\theta-\gamma T_c^TJ^{-T}(q)M(q)\dot q
\right)n_0f_d,\\
\dot{\hat\theta}
&=\gamma T_c^T\Bigl\{
\Lambda(q)\Bigl[
\ddot p_d+k_v\bigl(\dot p_d-J(q)\dot q\bigr)
+k_p\bigl(p_d-h(q)\bigr)
\Bigr]\\
&\qquad\qquad
+\dot\Lambda(q,\dot q)J(q)\dot q\\
&\qquad\qquad
+R_2\!\left(
\hat\theta-\gamma T_c^TJ^{-T}(q)M(q)\dot q
\right)n_0(f-f_d)
\Bigr\}.
\end{align}
$$
The only online measurements are $q,\dot q,f$; $p_d,\dot p_d,\ddot p_d,f_d$ are available reference signals, and $\hat\theta$ is the internal controller state. The known model functions are evaluated at these measured states, with
$$
\begin{align}
\Lambda(q)&=J^{-T}(q)M(q)J^{-1}(q),\\
\dot J(q,\dot q)&=\sum_{i=1}^{2}
\frac{\partial J(q)}{\partial q_i}\dot q_i,\\
\dot\Lambda(q,\dot q)&=\sum_{i=1}^{2}
\frac{\partial\Lambda(q)}{\partial q_i}\dot q_i.
\end{align}
$$
Thus the implementation requires no joint-acceleration measurement or numerical differentiation of the measured force. Initialize the internal estimate by
$$
\begin{align}
\hat\theta(0)
&=\bar\theta(0)+\gamma T_c^TJ^{-T}(q(0))M(q(0))\dot q(0).
\end{align}
$$
Then the closed loop has a unique forward-complete solution in the established-contact mode, all closed-loop states and commanded inputs are bounded, and the following properties hold for all $t\geq0$:

**(i) Angular-domain invariance and contact maintenance.** The effective estimate remains between its initial value and the true angle,
$$
\begin{align}
\bar\theta(t)&\in
\bigl[\min\{\bar\theta(0),\theta\},
\max\{\bar\theta(0),\theta\}\bigr]
\subseteq\mathcal I_c,\\
\phi(p(t))&=0,\qquad n^T\dot p(t)=0,\\
f(t)&\geq\rho_{\min}f_d>0.
\end{align}
$$
Thus unilateral contact persists without detachment.

**(ii) Exponential convergence of the effective parameter estimate.** With $z=\bar\theta-\theta$,
$$
\begin{align}
|z(t)|&\leq|z(0)|e^{-\alpha_z t},\\
\alpha_z&=\gamma\rho_{\min}f_d\cos\Delta>0.
\end{align}
$$
Consequently, $\bar n\to n$ exponentially. The manifold $\mathcal M=\{\hat\theta-\theta-\gamma T_c^Tm_p=0\}$ is invariant and exponentially attractive in the transverse coordinate $z$, and its internal dynamics realize FIC 1 with $k_f=0$. No persistence of excitation of the tangential motion is required.

**(iii) Exponential motion and force tracking.** Define the nominal motion decay rate by
$$
\begin{align}
A_m&=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\-k_p&-k_v\end{bmatrix},\\
\alpha_m&=-\max\{\operatorname{Re}\mu:\mu\in\operatorname{spec}(A_m)\}>0.
\end{align}
$$
For every $0<\alpha<\min\{\alpha_m,\alpha_z\}$, there exists a constant $C_\alpha>0$, independent of time, such that
$$
\begin{align}
\|e_p(t)\|+\|\dot e_p(t)\|
&\leq C_\alpha e^{-\alpha t}
\left(\|e_p(0)\|+\|\dot e_p(0)\|+|z(0)|\right),\\
|f(t)-f_d|
&\leq f_d\left(
\chi_\Lambda|z(0)|e^{-\alpha_z t}
+\frac{|z(0)|^2}{2}e^{-2\alpha_z t}
\right).
\end{align}
$$
Hence $p-p_d\to0$, $\dot p-\dot p_d\to0$, and $f-f_d\to0$ exponentially.

**(iv) Regulation and the internal estimate.** For a constant reference $p_d$, $\dot p\to0$, $\beta\to0$, and $\hat\theta\to\theta$. For a time-varying reference, the parameter convergence claim concerns $\bar\theta=\hat\theta+\beta$; the internal estimate $\hat\theta$ need not converge to $\theta$.

**Proof.** Using $p=h(q)$, $\dot p=J(q)\dot q$, $\Lambda J=J^{-T}M$, and the definition of $\eta$, the displayed torque law is exactly $\tau=J^T(\eta+\Lambda u_p-\bar n f_d)$. In the parameter update, substitution of $g_p=F-\eta+\dot\Lambda\dot p$ and $F-\eta=\Lambda u_p-\bar n f_d$ gives the displayed term $\Lambda u_p+\dot\Lambda J\dot q+\bar n(f-f_d)$. The expanded laws therefore coincide with IAC 1, and the preceding closed-loop identities apply.

On the active-contact branch, the reaction is uniquely determined because $n^T\Lambda^{-1}n>0$. The established coupling bound gives $\rho\geq\rho_{\min}>0$ whenever $\theta,\bar\theta\in\mathcal I_c$. Moreover,
$$
\begin{align}
\dot{\bar\theta}
&=-\gamma fH(\bar\theta-\theta),
\qquad \cos\Delta\leq H\leq1.
\end{align}
$$
The angular vector field points inward at both sector boundaries, and its sign keeps $\bar\theta$ between $\bar\theta(0)$ and $\theta$. At zero gap and zero normal velocity, the same command with zero reaction would produce normal acceleration $-\bigl(n^T\Lambda^{-1}n\bigr)\rho f_d<0$, which violates nonpenetration. The positive reaction instead enforces zero normal acceleration. The joint continuation argument in Invariance and Exponential Parameter Convergence therefore excludes both sector exit and contact loss, establishing (i).

With $V_z=z^2/2$, the bounds on $f$ and $H$ yield $\dot V_z\leq-2\alpha_zV_z$, which proves (ii). On $z=0$, $\bar n=n$ and $f=f_d$, so the force law and the dynamics restricted to $\mathcal M$ recover FIC 1; the full immersion identity is given in the appendix supplement.

For the motion error, let $y=[e_t\;\dot e_t]^T$. The actual error equation gives
$$
\begin{align}
\dot y&=A_my+
\begin{bmatrix}0\\-f_d\sin z/\Lambda_t\end{bmatrix},\\
\left|\frac{f_d}{\Lambda_t}\sin z(t)\right|
&\leq\frac{f_d}{\underline m}|z(0)|e^{-\alpha_z t}.
\end{align}
$$
Since $A_m$ is Hurwitz, variation of constants yields the stated estimate for every $\alpha<\min\{\alpha_m,\alpha_z\}$. The identities $e_p=Te_t$ and $\dot e_p=T\dot e_t$ transfer it to Cartesian coordinates. Substituting the bound on $z$ into $|f-f_d|\leq f_d(\chi_\Lambda|z|+z^2/2)$ gives the force estimate in (iii).

Bounded reference signals and the motion estimate imply bounded $p$ and $\dot p$. On the assumed compact nonsingular region, $\dot q=J^{-1}\dot p$, the robot model terms, $m_p$, $\beta$, and the commanded inputs are consequently bounded. The sector bound on $\bar\theta$ and $\hat\theta=\bar\theta-\beta$ also bound the estimator state. Smoothness and the positive contact margin permit continuation for all time under the operating-region assumption. Finally, for regulation, $\dot p\to0$ implies $\beta=-\gamma T_c^T\Lambda\dot p\to0$, giving (iv). $\square$

The confinement of $q$ to $\mathcal Q$ is an assumption of this theorem; invariance of the angular sector does not establish invariance of the robot operating region. Contact establishment, impacts, and contact-mode transitions remain outside its scope.

## Appendix

### IAC 1 Supplement: Immersion of the Target System

This appendix provides supplementary details for [[#IAC 1: Task-Space Control, Uncertain Surface Angle|IAC 1]].

The construction concerns the maintained unilateral-contact branch certified by the positive-force and invariance arguments of IAC 1.

In differential geometry context an immersion is a smooth map represents each trajectory of the possibly lower-dimensional FIC 1 target system as a trajectory of the actual adaptive closed loop on $\mathcal M$. The target system, the augmented closed loop, and the manifold are distinct objects, made explicit below.

#### Target and Augmented Closed-Loop Systems

Maintained rigid contact leaves one motion coordinate $x_t$ and its velocity $\dot x_t$. Fix a smooth inverse-kinematics branch in the nonsingular operating region and denote its contact-state chart by $\mathcal D_\theta\subset\mathbb R^2$. On this branch, write
$$
\begin{align}
q&=q_\theta(x_t),\qquad
h(q_\theta(x_t))=p_0+Tx_t,\\
\dot q&=J^{-1}(q_\theta(x_t))T\dot x_t.
\end{align}
$$
The subscript $\theta$ records the dependence of the contact geometry on the fixed true parameter. The target state is $\xi=[\xi_1\;\xi_2]^T\in\mathcal D_\theta$, representing the position and velocity of the full-information motion. Expressing the given reference as $x_{t,d}=T^T(p_d-p_0)$, define its nominal tangential acceleration by
$$
\begin{align}
u_{t,\star}(t,\xi_1,\xi_2)
&=\ddot x_{t,d}+k_v(\dot x_{t,d}-\xi_2)
+k_p(x_{t,d}-\xi_1).
\end{align}
$$
FIC 1 then has the two-dimensional target dynamics
$$
\begin{align}
\dot\xi&=\alpha(t,\xi)
=\begin{bmatrix}
\xi_2\\
u_{t,\star}(t,\xi_1,\xi_2)
\end{bmatrix},\qquad f_\star=f_d.
\end{align}
$$
This is the intrinsic-coordinate representation of the Cartesian target already specified in Target System. The explicit time dependence accommodates tracking; a constant reference gives the regulation case.

The actual closed-loop state includes the estimator:
$$
\begin{align}
X&=\begin{bmatrix}x_t&\dot x_t&\hat\theta\end{bmatrix}^T
\in\mathcal D_\theta\times\mathbb R,\\
\dot X&=\mathcal F_\theta(t,X)
=\begin{bmatrix}
\dot x_t\\
u_{t,\star}(t,x_t,\dot x_t)
+\dfrac{f_d}{\Lambda_t(q)}\sin z\\
\gamma T_c^T(g_p+\bar n f)
\end{bmatrix},\\
f&=\rho(q,z)f_d,\qquad
z=\hat\theta-\theta+\beta(q,\dot q).
\end{align}
$$
Here $q,\dot q$ are evaluated on the chosen branch, and $g_p,\bar n$ are the quantities defined by the implemented controller. Thus the actual augmented system is three-dimensional. The true angle $\theta$ is a constant parameter, and the contact force $f$ is determined algebraically by the rigid-contact constraint; neither is an additional dynamic state. The calligraphic $\mathcal F_\theta$ denotes the augmented vector field, whereas $F$ remains the Cartesian force command.

#### Immersion Map and the Manifold

Restrict the implemented correction to the contact-state chart:
$$
\begin{align}
\beta_\theta(\xi_1,\xi_2)
&=-\gamma T_c^T\Lambda(q_\theta(\xi_1))T\xi_2.
\end{align}
$$
For each fixed true angle $\theta$, define the map from target states to augmented states by
$$
\begin{align}
\pi_\theta:\mathcal D_\theta&\longrightarrow
\mathcal D_\theta\times\mathbb R,\\
\pi_\theta(\xi)
&=\begin{bmatrix}
\xi_1\\
\xi_2\\
\theta-\beta_\theta(\xi_1,\xi_2)
\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}
\xi_1\\
\xi_2\\
\theta+\gamma T_c^T\Lambda(q_\theta(\xi_1))T\xi_2
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{align}
$$
Its image is precisely the parameter manifold already defined:
$$
\begin{align}
\mathcal M
&=\left\{X:\hat\theta-\theta
+\beta_\theta(x_t,\dot x_t)=0\right\}
=\operatorname{im}\pi_\theta.
\end{align}
$$
Thus $\mathcal M$ is a two-dimensional graph in the three-dimensional augmented state space. It contains every admissible plant position and velocity paired with the internal estimate required to make $\bar\theta=\theta$. It includes target transients as well as the final regulated or tracked motion. This parameter manifold is distinct from the physical contact line $\mathcal S$ in the Cartesian workspace.

Writing $D\beta_\theta$ for the row derivative with respect to $(\xi_1,\xi_2)$, the derivative of the map is
$$
\begin{align}
D\pi_\theta(\xi)
&=\begin{bmatrix}I_2\\-D\beta_\theta(\xi)\end{bmatrix},
\qquad \operatorname{rank}D\pi_\theta(\xi)=2.
\end{align}
$$
It therefore is an immersion in the differential-geometric sense. Since the first two components recover $\xi$ uniquely, it is also an embedding on the chosen contact-state chart. The direction of the map is from the lower-dimensional target state to its representation on $\mathcal M$ inside the augmented closed loop. Although $\pi_\theta$ and $\mathcal M$ depend on the unknown true angle, they are analytical constructions; their evaluation is not required by the controller.

#### Dynamic Matching on the Manifold

Substituting $X=\pi_\theta(\xi)$ gives $z=0$, $\bar n=n$, and $\rho=1$. Hence $f=f_d$, the Cartesian force law is exactly FIC 1 with $k_f=0$, and the first two components of $\mathcal F_\theta(t,\pi_\theta(\xi))$ equal $\alpha(t,\xi)$. It remains to verify that the internal update supplies the third component required to stay on the graph.

Along a target trajectory, define $m_{p,\star}=\Lambda(q_\theta(\xi_1))T\xi_2$, and let $g_{p,\star}$ denote $g_p$ evaluated with the nominal force $F=\eta+\Lambda T u_{t,\star}-nf_d$. Then
$$
\begin{align}
\frac{d}{dt}m_{p,\star}
&=\dot\Lambda T\xi_2+\Lambda T u_{t,\star}
=g_{p,\star}+nf_d,\\
\frac{d}{dt}\left[\theta-\beta_\theta(\xi_1,\xi_2)\right]
&=-D\beta_\theta(\xi)\alpha(t,\xi)\\
&=\gamma T_c^T(g_{p,\star}+nf_d).
\end{align}
$$
The last expression is exactly the implemented $\dot{\hat\theta}$ on $\mathcal M$. Consequently, the complete vector fields satisfy the immersion, or dynamic-matching, equation
$$
\begin{align}
\mathcal F_\theta(t,\pi_\theta(\xi))
&=\begin{bmatrix}
\alpha(t,\xi)\\
-D\beta_\theta(\xi)\alpha(t,\xi)
\end{bmatrix}
=D\pi_\theta(\xi)\alpha(t,\xi).
\end{align}
$$
The map $\pi_\theta$ has no explicit time dependence, so this identity also applies to a time-varying reference. It states that evolving a target state and then mapping its trajectory gives the same result as evolving the actual augmented system from the mapped initial state. In particular,
$$
\begin{align}
X(0)=\pi_\theta(\xi(0))
\quad&\Longrightarrow\quad
X(t)=\pi_\theta(\xi(t)),\\
x_t(t)&=\xi_1(t),\qquad \dot x_t(t)=\xi_2(t),\\
\hat\theta(t)&=\theta+\gamma T_c^T\Lambda(q(t))\dot p(t).
\end{align}
$$
Thus the target dynamics are realized exactly by the actual closed loop restricted to $\mathcal M$. This is the specific dynamic-matching construction behind the direct I&I framework; see also [Liu, Ortega, Su, and Chu, ACC 2009, Section III](https://skoge.folk.ntnu.no/prost/proceedings/acc09/data/papers/0639.pdf).

#### Immersion, Invariance, and Attractivity

Immersion describes how target trajectories are represented in the augmented system. Invariance ensures that a trajectory initialized on $\mathcal M$ remains there and follows those target dynamics. Attraction is a separate property established by the preceding bound $|z(t)|\leq|z(0)|e^{-\alpha_z t}$ within the admissible angular domain. For a trajectory starting off $\mathcal M$, the plant motion initially contains the additional term $f_d\sin z/\Lambda_t$ and generally does not satisfy the target equation. As $z\to0$, this term and the force mismatch vanish; the subsequent motion and force tracking argument establishes convergence of the physical errors. Parameter-manifold attraction and physical tracking convergence are therefore distinct steps of the proof.

### IAC 1 Supplement: Numerical Example of Positive Contact Force Domain

Consider a constant Cartesian inertia and a desired contact force
$$
\begin{align}
\Lambda&=\begin{bmatrix}1.5&0.5\\0.5&1.5\end{bmatrix}\ \mathrm{kg},
\qquad f_d=10\ \mathrm{N}.
\end{align}
$$
The inertia eigenvalues are $1\ \mathrm{kg}$ and $2\ \mathrm{kg}$, so take $\underline m=1\ \mathrm{kg}$ and $\overline m=2\ \mathrm{kg}$. Choose $n_0=[0\;1]^T$, $\theta_c=0$, and $\Delta=\pi/6$ ($30^\circ$). Then
$$
\begin{align}
\mathcal I_c&=[-\pi/6,\pi/6],\\
\chi_\Lambda&=\frac{2-1}{2\sqrt{2\cdot1}}
=\frac{1}{2\sqrt2}\approx0.3536,\\
\rho_{\min}
&=\cos(\pi/3)-\frac{1}{2\sqrt2}\sin(\pi/3)
=\frac12-\frac{\sqrt6}{8}
\approx0.1938>0.
\end{align}
$$
Thus any true angle and initial effective estimate in this sector satisfy the sufficient angular condition. Their initial difference can be as large as $2\Delta=\pi/3$ ($60^\circ$), and the invariant-sector result guarantees
$$
\begin{align}
f(t)&\geq\rho_{\min}f_d\approx1.938\ \mathrm{N}.
\end{align}
$$
For a specific initial configuration, let the true surface be horizontal, $\theta=0$, and choose $\bar\theta(0)=-\pi/9$ ($-20^\circ$). Then $T=[1\;0]^T$, $n=[0\;1]^T$, $\Lambda_t=1.5\ \mathrm{kg}$, and $r_\Lambda=0.5/1.5=1/3$. The initial force is
$$
\begin{align}
\rho(q(0),z(0))
&=\cos(\pi/9)-\frac13\sin(\pi/9)
\approx0.8257,\\
f(0)&\approx8.257\ \mathrm{N}.
\end{align}
$$
The actual initial force is above the conservative sector-wide lower bound. As $z\to0$, $\rho\to1$ and $f\to10\ \mathrm{N}$. The true angle is specified here only to evaluate the example; its value is not supplied to the controller. Angles are expressed in radians in the control equations, with degrees included for interpretation.

### 2-DoF Ideal Decoupled Cartesian Manipulator

Consider an ideal Cartesian manipulator operating in the vertical plane, with two independent prismatic axes aligned with the horizontal first axis and the vertically upward second axis of $B$. The axes have constant effective masses $m_1>0$ and $m_2>0$. Transmission compliance, actuator dynamics, and internal friction are neglected; the contact force $F_e$ retains the interaction model defined above. Since the joints are prismatic, the symbols $\tau$ and $\tau_e$ denote generalized actuator and external forces, respectively, in the joint-space notation of the Robot Manipulator section.

#### Kinematics

Choose the joint-coordinate origins to coincide with the Cartesian origin, so that
$$
\begin{align}
q&=\begin{bmatrix}q_1\\q_2\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb R^2,\\
p&=h(q)=q,\\
\end{align}
$$
and
$$
\begin{align}
J(q)&=I_2,\qquad J^{-1}(q)=I_2,\qquad \dot J(q)=0.
\end{align}
$$
Consequently,
$$
\begin{align}
\dot p=\dot q,\qquad \ddot p=\ddot q.
\end{align}
$$
The Jacobian is nonsingular throughout the workspace. The fixed contact point $p_0$ remains the surface-frame origin and need not coincide with the Cartesian origin.

#### Joint-Space Dynamics

The constant diagonal inertia and the absence of velocity-dependent inertial terms give
$$
\begin{align}
M(q)&=\begin{bmatrix}m_1&0\\0&m_2\end{bmatrix},\\
C(q,\dot q)&=0_{2\times2}.
\end{align}
$$
With $g_B=[0\;-g]^T$ as defined above, the gravity vector for this ideal model is constant:
$$
\begin{align}
G(q)=G_0=-M g_B=\begin{bmatrix}0\\m_2g\end{bmatrix}.
\end{align}
$$
Gravity acts along the second axis, and the nonzero $G_0$ is retained explicitly and compensated using the known model.

The joint-space dynamics therefore reduces to
$$
\begin{align}
\begin{bmatrix}m_1&0\\0&m_2\end{bmatrix}\ddot q+\begin{bmatrix}0\\m_2g\end{bmatrix}
=\tau+\tau_e.
\end{align}
$$

#### Cartesian Dynamics

Substitution into the definitions of $\Lambda$, $\eta$, $F$, and $F_e$ yields
$$
\begin{align}
\Lambda(q)&=J^{-T}MJ^{-1}=M
=\begin{bmatrix}m_1&0\\0&m_2\end{bmatrix},\\
\eta(q,\dot q)&=J^{-T}(C\dot q+G)-\Lambda\dot J J^{-1}\dot p=G_0,\\
F&=J^{-T}\tau=\tau,\\
F_e&=J^{-T}\tau_e=\tau_e.
\end{align}
$$
Thus the task-space model has the same form as in the Robot Manipulator section,
$$
\begin{align}
\Lambda\ddot p+\eta=F+F_e,
\end{align}
$$
with constant diagonal $\Lambda$ and constant $\eta=G_0=[0\;m_2g]^T$. Writing $p=[p_1\;p_2]^T$, $F=[F_1\;F_2]^T$, and $F_e=[F_{e,1}\;F_{e,2}]^T$, the horizontal and vertical Cartesian channels are
$$
\begin{align}
m_1\ddot p_1&=F_1+F_{e,1},\\
m_2\ddot p_2+m_2g&=F_2+F_{e,2}.
\end{align}
$$
The inertia bounds hold globally with
$$
\begin{align}
\underline m&=\min\{m_1,m_2\}, \\
\overline m&=\max\{m_1,m_2\},\\
0&\prec\underline m I_2\preceq\Lambda\preceq\overline m I_2.
\end{align}
$$

### 2-DoF Planar Robot Arm Manipulator

Consider a serial two-link manipulator with two revolute joints operating in the vertical plane. The first joint is located at the origin of $B$, whose first axis is horizontal and whose second axis points vertically upward. Let $q=[q_1\;q_2]^T\in\mathbb R^2$, where $q_1$ is the counterclockwise angle of the first link from the positive horizontal axis and $q_2$ is the counterclockwise angle of the second link relative to the first link. Thus the absolute orientation of the second link is $q_1+q_2$.

For link $i\in\{1,2\}$, let $l_i>0$ be its length, $m_i>0$ its mass, $l_{c,i}\in(0,l_i]$ the distance from its proximal joint to its center of mass along the link, and $\mathcal I_i>0$ its moment of inertia about its center of mass for planar rotation. The links are rigid, and transmission compliance, actuator dynamics, and internal joint friction are neglected. All link parameters are assumed to be exactly known, consistently with the Robot Manipulator section.

#### Kinematics

The end-effector is located at the distal end of the second link, so the forward kinematics is
$$
\begin{align}
p=h(q)
=\begin{bmatrix}
l_1\cos q_1+l_2\cos(q_1+q_2)\\
l_1\sin q_1+l_2\sin(q_1+q_2)
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{align}
$$
The corresponding Jacobian is
$$
\begin{align}
J(q)=\frac{\partial h}{\partial q}
=\begin{bmatrix}
-l_1\sin q_1-l_2\sin(q_1+q_2)&-l_2\sin(q_1+q_2)\\
l_1\cos q_1+l_2\cos(q_1+q_2)&l_2\cos(q_1+q_2)
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{align}
$$
Thus
$$
\begin{align}
\dot p&=J(q)\dot q,\\
\ddot p&=J(q)\ddot q+\dot J(q)\dot q,
\end{align}
$$
where the velocity-dependent acceleration term is
$$
\begin{align}
\dot J(q)\dot q
=-\begin{bmatrix}
l_1\cos q_1\,\dot q_1^2+l_2\cos(q_1+q_2)(\dot q_1+\dot q_2)^2\\
l_1\sin q_1\,\dot q_1^2+l_2\sin(q_1+q_2)(\dot q_1+\dot q_2)^2
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{align}
$$
Since
$$
\begin{align}
\det J(q)=l_1l_2\sin q_2,
\end{align}
$$
the fully extended and folded configurations $q_2=k\pi$, $k\in\mathbb Z$, are singular. The operating region is chosen as a compact set on which $|\sin q_2|\geq\epsilon_J>0$, so that $J^{-1}(q)$ exists throughout the contact task. The surface-frame origin $p_0$ remains the first reliable contact point and need not coincide with the first joint.

#### Joint-Space Dynamics

Using the notation of the Robot Manipulator section, the dynamics is
$$
\begin{align}
M(q)\ddot q+C(q,\dot q)\dot q+G(q)=\tau+\tau_e,
\end{align}
$$
where $\tau=[\tau_1\;\tau_2]^T$ contains the actuator torques. The inertia matrix is
$$
\begin{align}
M(q)&=\begin{bmatrix}
M_{11}(q)&M_{12}(q)\\
M_{12}(q)&M_{22}
\end{bmatrix},\\
M_{11}(q)&=\mathcal I_1+\mathcal I_2+m_1l_{c,1}^2
+m_2\left(l_1^2+l_{c,2}^2+2l_1l_{c,2}\cos q_2\right),\\
M_{12}(q)&=\mathcal I_2+m_2\left(l_{c,2}^2+l_1l_{c,2}\cos q_2\right),\\
M_{22}&=\mathcal I_2+m_2l_{c,2}^2.
\end{align}
$$
The stated positive physical parameters ensure that $M(q)$ is symmetric positive definite.

Define
$$
\begin{align}
b(q_2)=m_2l_1l_{c,2}\sin q_2.
\end{align}
$$
A Coriolis and centrifugal matrix consistent with this inertia matrix is
$$
\begin{align}
C(q,\dot q)
=\begin{bmatrix}
-b(q_2)\dot q_2&-b(q_2)(\dot q_1+\dot q_2)\\
b(q_2)\dot q_1&0
\end{bmatrix},
\end{align}
$$
which gives
$$
\begin{align}
C(q,\dot q)\dot q
=\begin{bmatrix}
-b(q_2)(2\dot q_1\dot q_2+\dot q_2^2)\\
b(q_2)\dot q_1^2
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{align}
$$
With $g_B=[0\;-g]^T$ and the angles measured from the horizontal axis, the gravity vector on the left-hand side of the dynamics is
$$
\begin{align}
G(q)
=\begin{bmatrix}
(m_1l_{c,1}+m_2l_1)g\cos q_1+m_2l_{c,2}g\cos(q_1+q_2)\\
m_2l_{c,2}g\cos(q_1+q_2)
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{align}
$$
Thus $G(q)$ is configuration dependent and is retained in the model and gravity compensation.

#### Cartesian Dynamics

On the nonsingular operating region, the joint and Cartesian force variables satisfy
$$
\begin{align}
F&=J^{-T}(q)\tau,\qquad \tau=J^T(q)F,\\
F_e&=J^{-T}(q)\tau_e,\qquad \tau_e=J^T(q)F_e.
\end{align}
$$
Substituting the preceding $M$, $C$, $G$, and $J$ into the definitions in the Robot Manipulator section gives
$$
\begin{align}
\Lambda(q)&=J^{-T}(q)M(q)J^{-1}(q),\\
\eta(q,\dot q)&=J^{-T}(q)\left(C(q,\dot q)\dot q+G(q)\right)
-\Lambda(q)\dot J(q)\dot q.
\end{align}
$$
The expression for $\eta$ is equivalent to the general definition because $\dot q=J^{-1}(q)\dot p$. Consequently,
$$
\begin{align}
\Lambda(q)\ddot p+\eta(q,\dot q)=F+F_e.
\end{align}
$$
The Cartesian inertia is generally configuration dependent and nondiagonal. Since $M(q)\succ0$ and $J(q)$ is nonsingular, $\Lambda(q)\succ0$; continuity on the chosen compact operating region yields the uniform inertia bounds assumed above.

The contact decomposition $F_e=nf+Tf_t$, the surface coordinates, and the motion-force controller therefore apply with these $\Lambda$ and $\eta$. The actuator torques are obtained from $\tau=J^TF$, with the configuration-dependent gravity contribution included through $\eta$.

#### An Explicit Realization

We now give an explicit realization of the preceding full-information controller for the contact task in the vertical plane. Assume that the true geometry $n$, $T=T(n)$, and $Q=TT^{T}$ are known. Since the contact surface is fixed, $T$ is constant.

##### Controller

When tangential contact force is present, $f_t$ is also assumed to be known in this full-information realization. For frictionless contact, $f_t=0$.

Define the Cartesian and intrinsic motion errors as
$$
\begin{align}
e_p & = p-p_d \in\mathbb{R}^{2}, \\
e_t & = x_{t}-x_{t,d} \in\mathbb{R}.
\end{align}
$$
Since both $p$ and $p_d$ lie on the true contact surface,
$$
\begin{align}
e_p=Te_t,
\qquad
Qe_p=e_p.
\end{align}
$$
Define the reference Cartesian velocity as
$$
\begin{align}
v_r
&=
\dot p_d-k_pQe_p \\
&=
T\left(\dot x_{t,d}-k_pe_t\right),
\qquad
k_p>0.
\end{align}
$$
Equivalently, $v_r=T(\dot x_{t,d}+k_px_{t,d})-k_p(p-p_0)$. This expression can be evaluated using the estimated tangent in the adaptive-control framework below.
The filtered Cartesian motion error is
$$
\begin{align}
s=\dot p-v_r.
\end{align}
$$
Because both $\dot p$ and $v_r$ are tangential, $s$ admits the intrinsic representation
$$
\begin{align}
s&=Ts_t, \\
s_t&=\dot e_t+k_pe_t
\in\mathbb{R}.
\end{align}
$$
Choose the tangential acceleration command as
$$
\begin{align}
u_m
&=
T^{T}\left(\dot v_r-k_ss\right) \\
&=
\ddot x_{t,d}-k_p\dot e_t-k_ss_t,
\qquad
k_s>0.
\end{align}
$$
It follows that
$$
\begin{align}
\dot v_r-k_ss=Tu_m.
\end{align}
$$

For the normal-force channel, the feedforward full-information choice is $u_f=f_d$. If exact scalar force feedback is available, so that $f_m=f$, define
$$
\begin{align}
e_f=f_d-f_m
\end{align}
$$
and optionally use
$$
\begin{align}
u_f
=
f_d+k_fe_f,
\qquad
k_f\geq0.
\end{align}
$$

Substituting these commands into the preceding nominal controller gives
$$
\begin{align}
F
=
\eta
+\Lambda\left(\dot v_r-k_ss\right)
-Tf_t
-nu_f.
\end{align}
$$
When contact is frictionless, $f_t=0$, and the controller reduces to
$$
\begin{align}
F
=
\eta
+\Lambda\left(\dot v_r-k_ss\right)
-nu_f.
\end{align}
$$


##### Closed-Loop System

Substituting the controller and the contact-force decomposition $F_e=nf+Tf_t$ into the task-space dynamics gives
$$
\begin{align}
\Lambda\ddot p+\eta
&=
\eta
+\Lambda\left(\dot v_r-k_ss\right)
-Tf_t
-nu_f
+nf
+Tf_t.
\end{align}
$$
Using $s=\dot p-v_r$, and hence $\dot s=\ddot p-\dot v_r$, and canceling the exactly known $\eta$ including gravity, the closed-loop system becomes
$$
\begin{align}
\Lambda\dot s
=
-k_s\Lambda s
+n(f-u_f).
\end{align}
$$
Since $s=Ts_t$ and $T$ is constant, left multiplication by $T^{T}$ yields
$$
\begin{align}
\Lambda_t\dot s_t
=
-k_s\Lambda_t s_t,
\qquad
\Lambda_t=T^{T}\Lambda T>0.
\end{align}
$$
Therefore,
$$
\begin{align}
\dot s_t=-k_ss_t.
\end{align}
$$
Together with the definition of $u_m$, this relation is equivalent to $\ddot x_{t}=u_m$.
It follows that $\dot s=-k_ss$. Substitution into the Cartesian closed-loop system then gives
$$
\begin{align}
n(f-u_f)=0,
\end{align}
$$
and, since $\|n\|=1$,
$$
\begin{align}
f=u_f.
\end{align}
$$
Thus the explicit realization recovers the scalar tangential-motion and normal-force target dynamics.

##### Position and Force Tracking

From the definition $s_t=\dot e_t+k_pe_t$, the intrinsic position-error dynamics is
$$
\begin{align}
\dot e_t=-k_pe_t+s_t.
\end{align}
$$
Combining the position-error and filtered-error dynamics gives
$$
\begin{align}
\begin{bmatrix}
\dot e_t\\
\dot s_t
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
-k_p&1\\
0&-k_s
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
e_t\\
s_t
\end{bmatrix}.
\end{align}
$$
The eigenvalues are $-k_p$ and $-k_s$. Hence $e_t$ and $s_t$ converge exponentially to zero. Since $e_p=Te_t$ and $T^{T}T=1$, the Cartesian position error $e_p$ also converges exponentially to zero.

For the feedforward force command $u_f=f_d$, the closed-loop relation $f=u_f$ directly gives
$$
\begin{align}
f=f_d.
\end{align}
$$
If exact scalar force feedback is used instead, then $f_m=f$ and
$$
\begin{align}
f
=u_f
=f_d+k_f(f_d-f).
\end{align}
$$
Therefore,
$$
\begin{align}
(1+k_f)(f-f_d)=0.
\end{align}
$$
Since $k_f\geq0$, it follows that $f=f_d$. Consequently, the realization achieves exponential position tracking and exact normal-force tracking for the contact task in the vertical plane. It also makes explicit which true-geometry quantities must be replaced by their estimated counterparts in the adaptive-control framework developed next.

## Adaptive Control Framework

This section introduces the classical adaptive-control framework for the vertical-plane contact task, in which the geometry estimate $\hat n$ is used directly in the controller while the exactly known robot dynamics, including $G(q)$, remain accounted for through $\eta$.

### Estimated Geometry

The classical adaptive controller uses the geometry estimate
$$
\begin{align}
\hat n\in S^{1}.
\end{align}
$$
Define the estimated projector and tangent basis as
$$
\begin{align}
\hat Q&=I_2-\hat n\hat n^{T}, \\
\hat T&=T(\hat n)=R_{\text{-90}}\hat n\in\mathbb R^2.
\end{align}
$$
The adaptive design must provide the regularity needed to compute $\dot{\hat T}$ and $\ddot{\hat T}$.

The estimated intrinsic coordinate and workspace reference are
$$
\begin{align}
\hat x_{t}&=\hat T^{T}(p-p_{0})\in\mathbb R, \\
\hat p_d&=p_{0}+\hat T x_{t,d}.
\end{align}
$$
These quantities rely only on known or computable values, unlike $x_{t}=T^{T}(p-p_{0})$, which depends on the unknown true geometry. Since $\hat T$ varies during adaptation,
$$
\begin{align}
\dot{\hat p}_d
&=
\hat T\dot x_{t,d}+\dot{\hat T}x_{t,d}, \\
\ddot{\hat p}_d
&=
\hat T\ddot x_{t,d}+2\dot{\hat T}\dot x_{t,d}+\ddot{\hat T}x_{t,d}.
\end{align}
$$
Therefore, the geometry-induced reference derivative terms must be retained in the classical adaptive-controller design.
